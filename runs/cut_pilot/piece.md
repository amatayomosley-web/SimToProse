# The Right Way Now

The yarrow came in sparse that year.

Maren crouched at the frost-edge of the upper meadow and counted the sprigs as she dropped them into the basket — one bundle, two — and the counting was not superstition but arithmetic: three times this much, she'd need, at minimum, to carry Ashford through a hard winter, and three times this much was already more than the lower bank had left. *Won't be enough,* she thought, straightening with her hands on her knees. *It's never enough.*

She went home and hung what she had.

---

Bryn was six years old and burning when they carried him through her door.

She was already moving before the door was fully open — one arm cleared the cot, she pointed without looking: *Set her down. Gently. Head up.* The back of her wrist to the forehead, then the throat, fingers counting the pulse at the hollow of the neck. Too fast. Too warm already.

*Don't go to that place. Look at her. Just look.*

She worked the night and the next. On the second night, wringing the cloth in a basin gone lukewarm, she caught herself thinking: *Second night. It was the second night with Súil too.* She pressed her lips together. That is not this. Count the steps. Willow bark, one measure. Fever hasn't broken which means the root cause is still — one, two — the root cause is still there and countable and she would count it.

Her apprentice Joss appeared in the doorway at the third watch. He was a careful young man and gifted; she had thought so since his first year. He said he could take the night. She set down the compress she was wringing and did not look at him.

"No." Flat. No softening. She picked up the compress and wrung it again — once, twice — then added, quieter: "Bryn's interval is wrong and I don't know yet what it means." She would sleep in the chair.

She did not explain that trusting another with a life was not something she knew anymore how to do. She had known, once. That was before.

The third night she moved the lamp to the child's face. Not for herself — she could read a fever in the dark by now — but because she needed to see. *I know what third nights do.* One, two — *drink it* — three. The bark's had long enough to draw. If it was going to hold, it held by morning.

Dawn came without frost. She stood at the window with the shutter open a finger's width and counted the jars on the shelf — one, two, three — not because she didn't know how many there were. The night had held. Everyone the night held was still there. That was enough. That was enough for now.

When Bryn's fever broke with the light, she stood at the bedside a long moment with her hand flat to his forehead. Then she exhaled — one slow breath through her nose — and wrung out the cloth, folded it, set it on the basin edge. She lifted his wrist and counted the pulse and her fingers were very steady.

*Not enough.* The thought arrived clean as water, as it always did. *It was not enough last time and I knew it and this time I did not sleep. Four days. I counted every dose. I did not sleep. He is warm the right way now.*

He was warm the right way now.

She lay down on the narrow cot in the back room, still in her apron, and put one hand on the shelf beside her where the fever-bark was kept. She did not quite sleep. But she counted, under her breath, and kept counting, and somewhere in the middle of it her eyes closed.

---

Tobin was sixty-three years old and had needed her every winter for the last eight years. Warmth one winter, fennel for the gut in spring, the chest poultice the year before last. She had kept him. She had kept him that long.

She pulled the blanket up close to his chin — not tucking, just resting her hand there a moment. "Tobin. I've got you." She counted the drops of valerian into the cup: one, two, three, four, five. *That's right. That's enough.* She did not think about what enough meant tonight.

She had four days with him and she used every hour of them. The dosing was right. The poultice was right. On the fourth night she sat beside him and counted the interval between his breaths, and the intervals grew, and she sat with it, and she did not leave. When the candle had burned down a finger-width she laid his hand across his chest and tucked the blanket edge under so the cold wouldn't settle in while it was still worth minding. She went to the window and stood there in the dark.

*The dosing was right. One, two — I did not miss the turn of it. I did not miss it. He needed more time and I could not make more time.*

In the morning she brought the basin and the cloth and washed him the way her mother had shown her, starting at the feet and working upward — one stroke, two, three — the way she always did, the old rite, the only rite she knew for the ones who didn't come back. Her lips moved through it without sound. The fever, she thought, had gone east when she'd thought it went west. She should have tried the willow bark earlier. She had yarrow enough — she had yarrow enough and she'd held the boil too long on the second night, and the volatile oils go off when you hold the heat, and she had always known that, she had always known that, and still.

At the square she stood at the edge with her back half-turned to the crowd and pressed her fingers against the hem of her apron, counting the folds. One. Two. Three. She did not join the weeping. When the miller's wife touched her arm she nodded and moved away.

---

Joss found her in the workroom that afternoon.

He stood in the doorway — the same doorway, she thought, where he'd offered to take the night watch for Bryn — and he asked her if he could have done more. His voice was careful with it.

The counting stopped. The pestle went down, too carefully.

She did not look at him for a long time. Then, without turning: "You got him to me before the third day. That's what you could do, and you did it."

A silence.

*He's asking me to give him the weight of it. I won't. I carry that. That's mine.*

"Go home, Joss."

He went. She picked up the pestle and set it down again. Reached for the valerian on the shelf — moved it to the left of the hyssop, then to the right, then back. Lifted it a third time. Set it left. Said under her breath: "Fever-root, willow-bark, valerian, yarrow. Fever-root, willow-bark, valerian, yarrow." If everything was in order when they came — when someone came — she would not lose a minute. One minute. She would not lose one minute looking.

---

The first hard frost came before dawn.

Maren stood at the workbench with the door at her back, counting under her breath — one, two, three — the bundles of dried yarrow hanging from the rafter. She reached up and touched the nearest one, checking the stem for damp. Moved to the willow bark. She needed more willow bark. The Ferris boy had been coughing since yesterday and she had said nothing to his mother, because if she said something she would have to begin again, and she would begin again.

She turned to the window. The frost lay on the glass in its fine particular patterns, and below it the village was quiet, and she counted what she had, and it was not enough, and she counted it again.
