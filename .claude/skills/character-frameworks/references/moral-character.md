# Moral Character and Virtue

The ethical layer — what a person counts as right, what they will and won't do, and which way they break when a scene forces the choice. This is not a separate faculty bolted onto personality; in the repo's generative model virtue is **not stored** but **generated** — "courage" is FEAR active with a stronger primary (SEEKING, CARE, RAGE) overriding it, resolved; "betrayal" is self-interested SEEKING beating CARE; the coward and the hero feel identical fear and differ only in the competing pull's weight or fear's gain (`docs/generative-model.md`). So this file is not a menu of moral traits to set. It is the well of *observers' theories* of moral character — the frameworks that name the patterns the generator produces, catalogue the virtues and vices a culture recognizes, chart how moral reasoning matures, and explain how a person of ordinary conscience talks themselves into an atrocity. The repo's own moral spine is already fixed: **Moral Foundations** (Haidt's six taste-buds) is the ethics lens over Schwartz's value spine (`docs/values-and-stakes.md`); **Honesty-Humility** is the HEXACO factor that makes integrity-vs-manipulation a first-class trait dimension (`docs/trait-theory.md`); the **seven Panksepp primaries** are the affective basis virtues are blended from (`docs/generative-model.md`); and **orientation** (locus of control, coping) plus the **goals/fears/wounds** of the drives schema supply the operands a moral decision resolves (`docs/drives-schema.md`). Those are the reference defaults. This file surrounds them with the wider library — Aristotle's mean, the VIA strengths, Kohlberg and Gilligan and Turiel and Rest on moral development, the social-intuitionist and moral-disengagement accounts of how judgment actually happens, the self-conscious moral emotions (shame vs guilt) that are the reaction *beneath* the reasoning, and the scholarly fight over whether "moral character" is a trait, a social-cognitive system, or a lived practice of virtue at all. Two working cautions run through everything below. First, a *moral label is an observer's summary of a resolved collision, never a field* — the same discipline the repo applies to every trait. Second, **virtue on the page is the vice next door held in check** — the honest person tempted, the brave one afraid, the just one who wants revenge — because a virtue with no opposing pull is not a character, it is a mascot.

## Aristotelian virtue ethics and the golden mean

**Name and lineage.** Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* (c. 340 BCE), building on Plato and Socrates; the founding text of the Western virtue tradition, revived in the twentieth century by G. E. M. Anscombe ("Modern Moral Philosophy," 1958), Philippa Foot, Alasdair MacIntyre (*After Virtue*, 1981), and Rosalind Hursthouse. The dominant rival to the two great *act*-focused ethical theories (Kantian duty, utilitarian consequence): where they ask "what is the right *act*," virtue ethics asks "what is the good *person*, and what would they do."

**Core idea.** Moral virtue is not a rule you follow but a **stable disposition of character** (Greek *hexis* — a settled state, more than a habit, less than a passing mood) to feel and act well, acquired by *practice*, the way a builder becomes a builder by building. Each virtue is a **mean between two vices** — an excess and a deficiency — of some feeling or action; courage sits between cowardice (too much fear, too little confidence) and rashness (too little fear). The mean is **not arithmetic** but "relative to us" and to the situation: the right amount of anger, "at the right times, toward the right people, for the right reason, in the right way" — which is why the doctrine is *not* a bland recommendation of moderation. Getting the mean right in the particular case requires **phronesis** (practical wisdom) — the perceptual, experienced judgment that reads what *this* situation demands. The end (*telos*) of all virtue is **eudaimonia**: flourishing, "living well," the activity of the soul in accordance with virtue over a complete life — not a feeling but a *way of living*.

**Structure in full.** The core apparatus:

- **The Doctrine of the Mean.** Every sphere of feeling/action admits an excess, a deficiency, and an intermediate; the virtue is the intermediate, hit consistently and for the right reasons. Some acts (murder, adultery, theft) have *no* mean — they are wrong in themselves.
- **Aristotle's table of virtues and vices** (the canonical list, each a mean between two named vices):
  - *Fear and confidence* → **Courage**, between **Cowardice** (deficiency of confidence / excess of fear) and **Rashness** (excess of confidence).
  - *Pleasures and pains* → **Temperance**, between **Insensibility** (too little) and **Licentiousness / self-indulgence** (too much).
  - *Giving and taking money, small scale* → **Liberality (generosity)**, between **Illiberality / meanness** and **Prodigality (wastefulness)**.
  - *Giving and taking money, grand scale* → **Magnificence**, between **Pettiness / stinginess** and **Vulgarity / tastelessness**.
  - *Honour, large scale* → **Magnanimity (greatness of soul)**, between **Pusillanimity (smallness of soul)** and **Vanity / empty conceit**.
  - *Honour, small scale* → **Proper ambition**, between **Unambitiousness** and **Over-ambition**.
  - *Anger* → **Patience / good temper**, between **Lack of spirit** and **Irascibility (short temper)**.
  - *Truth about oneself* → **Truthfulness**, between **Self-deprecation / understatement (irony)** and **Boastfulness**.
  - *Amusement / conversation* → **Wittiness**, between **Boorishness** and **Buffoonery**.
  - *Social conduct / friendliness* → **Friendliness**, between **Surliness / quarrelsomeness** and **Obsequiousness / flattery**.
  - *Shame* (a semi-virtue of feeling) → **Modesty**, between **Shamelessness** and **Shyness / bashfulness**.
  - *Others' fortunes* → **Righteous indignation**, between **Spitefulness / malice** and **Envy**.
- **Justice** gets its own book (Book V) as a special, "complete" virtue — both a general virtue (lawfulness, the whole of virtue toward others) and a particular one (fairness in distribution and rectification).
- **The intellectual virtues** (of the reasoning part of the soul, acquired by teaching, not habit): *sophia* (theoretical wisdom), *episteme* (scientific knowledge), *nous* (intuitive understanding of first principles), *techne* (craft/art), and — the one that governs moral action — **phronesis** (practical wisdom). Moral virtue without phronesis is blind; phronesis without moral virtue is mere cleverness. You cannot be fully good in one virtue without the practical wisdom that unifies them (the *unity of the virtues*).
- **The continence spectrum.** Aristotle grades moral states: the **virtuous** person does right *and wants to* (no inner conflict); the **continent** (*enkratic*) person does right but against contrary desire (white-knuckle willpower); the **incontinent** (*akratic*) person knows the good but is mastered by appetite and does wrong (weakness of will — *akrasia*); the **vicious** person does wrong and feels fine about it. This four-step ladder is pure gold for character interiority: the drama of temptation *is* the gap between virtuous and continent.
- **Habituation.** Virtue is *learned by doing* — "we become just by doing just acts, brave by doing brave acts." Character is the sediment of repeated choice; a formed vice is as stable as a formed virtue and as hard to reverse.

**Vivid exemplars.** Atticus Finch as phronesis incarnate — the practically wise man reading exactly what each situation demands. Aragorn as magnanimity (greatness of soul worn lightly). Odysseus as the *phronimos*, cunning wisdom applied. Boromir as the collapse of continence into akrasia — he *knows* the Ring is poison and is mastered anyway. Ned Stark as rigid virtue *without* phronesis — honest and just, but lacking the practical wisdom to read the court that kills him, a cautionary tale that virtue-without-prudence is not enough. Aristotle's own villains map onto the excess pole: Achilles is courage curdled toward rashness and honour toward vanity; Scrooge is illiberality (the money-deficiency vice) before his conversion, prodigality never his temptation.

**What it gives you for character depth.** The single most useful craft tool in this file: **every virtue is drawn with its two flanking vices**, so you always know the *two* ways a good trait fails — and a character is most alive teetering toward one of them. It supplies the interiority of *temptation* (the continent/akratic distinction: doing right against desire is more dramatic than doing right easily) and the concept of *practical wisdom* — the difference between a person who has the right values and one who can actually *read the room and act*, which is often the whole of a competence arc. Because virtue is *habituated*, it gives a mechanism for change over a life: character as the accumulation of choices, alterable only slowly and by practice — the shape of a real arc, not a switch.

**Combines-with.** The mean's "two vices per virtue" is the deep structure behind the repo's whole-trait tails: a HEXACO facet drawn at *both* extremes with the failure mode at each pole (`references/trait-models.md`) is Aristotle's excess/deficiency in psychometric dress. **Phronesis** is the naturalistic-decision competence of `emotion-and-decision` (Klein's recognition-primed expertise is phronesis for firefighters). **Akrasia** is a hot-cold or present-bias override in that same toolbox — the somatic pull beating the endorsed value. Eudaimonia-as-flourishing maps onto Self-Determination Theory's autonomy/competence/relatedness in the worth menu. The virtues themselves cross-tabulate with the VIA strengths below (which explicitly revived Aristotle) and with Moral Foundations (justice ≈ fairness; the loyalty/friendliness virtues ≈ in-group). In the generative model, a virtue-as-mean *is* the crossover point where the situation drives the collision margin to zero (`docs/generative-model.md`) — "brave to a point" is the cost level where FEAR finally wins.

**Cautions.** (1) *The mean is not moderation* — a lukewarm character who splits every difference is a misreading; the mean is the *right* amount, which at the extreme (facing a tyrant) may be maximal defiance. (2) *Cultural loading* — Aristotle's specific list (magnificence, magnanimity) encodes the values of a slave-holding Athenian aristocracy; the *structure* travels, the *contents* are parochial. (3) *The unity-of-virtues thesis* (you can't have one fully without all) makes for implausibly seamless saints; real and interesting characters have virtues that *conflict* (a person courageous but cruel, just but cold), which is exactly the drama the repo prizes — so treat unity as an ideal limit, not a design rule. (4) The situationist critique (below) argues the stable cross-situational *hexis* Aristotle assumes may not empirically exist.

**Sources.** Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* (esp. Books II–VI); Rosalind Hursthouse, *On Virtue Ethics* (1999); Alasdair MacIntyre, *After Virtue* (1981); Julia Annas, *Intelligent Virtue* (2011); Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Aristotle's Ethics."

## The VIA Classification of Character Strengths and Virtues

**Name and lineage.** Christopher **Peterson** & Martin **Seligman**, *Character Strengths and Virtues: A Handbook and Classification* (2004) — the flagship taxonomy of positive psychology, deliberately built as a "manual of the sanities" to counterweight the DSM's manual of disorders. Backed by the VIA Institute on Character and the VIA-IS survey (millions of respondents). Derived by surveying the virtue lists of the world's philosophical and religious traditions (Aristotle, Aquinas, Confucius, the Bhagavad Gita, the Bushido code, etc.) and distilling what recurred.

**Core idea.** Six **core virtues** appear near-universally across cultures and eras; each is reached through a set of measurable, trainable **character strengths** — 24 in all — that are the psychological *routes* to the virtues. A strength counts if it is valued in its own right (not just for outcomes), morally toned, doesn't diminish others, has an "unfelt" opposite (a genuine vice), and is embodied in cultural paragons. Each person has a profile of **signature strengths** (their top few, felt as authentic and energizing).

**Structure in full.** Six virtues, 24 strengths:

- **Wisdom and Knowledge** (cognitive strengths — acquiring and using information):
  - **Creativity** — original, adaptive ideas; novel ways to do things.
  - **Curiosity** — interest in ongoing experience; openness, exploration, fascination.
  - **Judgment / Open-Mindedness** — thinking things through, weighing evidence, not jumping to conclusions.
  - **Love of Learning** — mastering new skills and bodies of knowledge for its own sake.
  - **Perspective (Wisdom)** — able to give wise counsel; seeing the large pattern, the whole.
- **Courage** (emotional strengths — exercising will to reach goals against opposition):
  - **Bravery** — not shrinking from threat, challenge, pain; speaking up for what's right.
  - **Perseverance** — finishing what one starts; persistence despite obstacles.
  - **Honesty / Authenticity** — speaking truth, presenting oneself genuinely, no pretense.
  - **Zest** — vitality, energy, enthusiasm; living life as an adventure.
- **Humanity** (interpersonal strengths — tending and befriending):
  - **Love** — valuing close relations; capacity for mutual caring intimacy.
  - **Kindness** — generosity, nurturance, care, compassion; doing good deeds.
  - **Social Intelligence** — awareness of the motives and feelings of self and others; reading rooms.
- **Justice** (civic strengths — healthy community life):
  - **Teamwork / Citizenship** — loyalty, doing one's share, social responsibility.
  - **Fairness** — treating all by principles of justice; not letting bias distort decisions.
  - **Leadership** — organizing and motivating a group to get things done and keep good relations.
- **Temperance** (strengths that protect against excess):
  - **Forgiveness / Mercy** — accepting others' shortcomings; letting go of the impulse to punish.
  - **Humility / Modesty** — letting accomplishments speak for themselves; not seeking the spotlight.
  - **Prudence** — careful about choices; not saying or doing what one might regret.
  - **Self-Regulation / Self-Control** — governing one's appetites, emotions, impulses.
- **Transcendence** (strengths that connect to the larger universe and give meaning):
  - **Appreciation of Beauty and Excellence** — awe and wonder at skill, beauty, moral goodness.
  - **Gratitude** — awareness of and thankfulness for the good.
  - **Hope / Optimism** — expecting the best and working to achieve it.
  - **Humor / Playfulness** — bringing lightness; seeing the funny side.
  - **Spirituality / Religiousness / Purpose** — coherent beliefs about meaning and one's place in the cosmos.

**Vivid exemplars.** Signature-strength portraits: Hermione Granger (love of learning + judgment + bravery); Samwise Gamgee (loyalty/teamwork + hope + kindness — the humblest strengths as the load-bearing ones); Ted Lasso (zest + kindness + social intelligence + hope, weaponized); Atticus Finch (fairness + bravery + perspective); Leslie Knope (perseverance + leadership + zest); Marcus Aurelius (self-regulation + humility + perspective, in the *Meditations*); Baloo (playfulness + zest); Andy Dufresne in *Shawshank* (hope + perseverance as literal survival). A signature-strength *overused* becomes a flaw: Javert is fairness/justice ossified into cruelty; Boxer in *Animal Farm* is perseverance without judgment.

**What it gives you for character depth.** A rich, *positive* vocabulary — 24 nameable goods — that most trait models lack, letting you specify what a character is *good at* being, not only their neuroses. The **signature strengths** idea gives a fast, warm way to build a likable protagonist (find their top three, put them under pressure) and a mechanism for authenticity (a person acting *outside* their signature strengths feels drained and false — usable as an arc of self-betrayal). Overuse/underuse of a strength recovers the Aristotelian excess/deficiency for free, so each strength doubles as a flaw generator.

**Combines-with.** The six virtues are a deliberate re-derivation of Aristotle + the cardinal/theological virtues + Confucius, so it cross-links to every virtue system here. Individual strengths map onto HEXACO facets (Honesty ≈ H-Sincerity/Fairness; Prudence/Self-Regulation ≈ C-Prudence; Curiosity/Creativity ≈ Openness; Bravery inversely ≈ Emotionality-Fearfulness; Zest ≈ Extraversion-Liveliness) and onto Moral Foundations (Fairness↔fairness, Teamwork↔loyalty, Spirituality↔sanctity). As signature strengths they can *be* the drives-schema self-image ("someone who never gives up" = a perseverance-anchored identity, `docs/drives-schema.md`). Read the strength as an emergent name for a generator setting, never a stored field — VIA is a *label vocabulary*, the exact thing the repo says virtues are.

**Cautions.** (1) *Self-report and desirability* — the VIA-IS measures who you'd *like* to be as much as who you are; a character's *self*-view of their strengths (drives-schema `view: self`) can diverge sharply from truth, which is richer than treating the profile as ground truth. (2) *Positivity bias* — 24 goods and no systematic vice taxonomy; pair it with the seven deadly sins or Aristotle's excess-poles to get the shadow. (3) The six-virtue factor structure is *empirically shaky* — factor analyses often recover 3–5 factors, not 6, and strengths cross-load; treat the grouping as a useful humanistic scaffold, not a validated dimensional model. (4) *Cultural framing* — despite the cross-cultural sourcing, the instrument and its individualist "signature strength / authenticity" framing are Western-positive-psychology inflected.

**Sources.** Peterson & Seligman, *Character Strengths and Virtues: A Handbook and Classification* (2004); Niemiec, *Character Strengths Interventions* (2018); McGrath, "Measurement invariance in the VIA" (2016); Ruch et al., "Mapping strengths into virtues" (*Frontiers in Psychology*, 2015); viacharacter.org.

## Kohlberg's stages of moral development

**Name and lineage.** Lawrence **Kohlberg** (Harvard, 1958 dissertation onward), extending Jean Piaget's cognitive-developmental account of moral judgment. The dominant paradigm in moral psychology for a generation; assessed via the **Moral Judgment Interview** and hypothetical dilemmas, most famously the **Heinz dilemma** (a man whose wife is dying — should he steal the overpriced drug that could save her?). Kohlberg's key move: score the *reasoning*, not the verdict — two people who both say "steal it" can be at wildly different stages.

**Core idea.** Moral reasoning develops through an **invariant sequence of six stages** (grouped in three levels), each a qualitatively distinct *structure* of justification, each more adequate than the last at handling conflicting claims. Movement is one-way, no skipping; higher stages are triggered by cognitive conflict (encountering reasoning one stage above your own). Most adults stabilize at the conventional level; postconventional reasoning is comparatively rare.

**Structure in full.** Three levels, two stages each:

- **Level 1 — Preconventional** (morality is external; the self's interests are primary):
  - **Stage 1 — Obedience and Punishment.** Right = avoiding punishment; rules are fixed and obeyed because authority backs them with force. "Don't steal — you'll go to jail." Might makes right.
  - **Stage 2 — Instrumental / Self-Interest ("What's in it for me?").** Right = serving one's own needs, with a crude reciprocity ("you scratch my back"). Deals, fair exchange, but ego-centered. "Steal it if he wants her alive — that's his business."
- **Level 2 — Conventional** (morality = upholding the expectations of one's group and society):
  - **Stage 3 — Interpersonal / "Good Boy–Nice Girl."** Right = being liked and approved, living up to what those close to you expect; motives and being "nice" matter. "A good husband would steal it — everyone would think him heartless otherwise."
  - **Stage 4 — Law and Order / Social System.** Right = maintaining the social order, doing your duty, respecting law and authority for the sake of the whole system, not just your circle. "Stealing is against the law — but the druggist is wrong too; still, order must hold."
- **Level 3 — Postconventional / Principled** (morality = self-chosen universal principles that can stand *against* law and convention):
  - **Stage 5 — Social Contract and Individual Rights.** Laws are social agreements for mutual benefit, changeable by democratic process; some values (life, liberty) precede any law. "The law didn't anticipate this; the right to life outweighs the druggist's property right."
  - **Stage 6 — Universal Ethical Principles.** Right = self-chosen, universalizable principles of justice, equality, and the dignity of persons (Kantian in flavor); when law violates these, one follows conscience and accepts the penalty (civil disobedience). "A human life is worth more than property, universally — Heinz *must* steal it, and stand for it."

(Kohlberg speculated, but never validated, a mystical/cosmic **Stage 7**.)

**Vivid exemplars.** Stage 1: a small child, or a brute who only fears the whip. Stage 2: Han Solo at his first appearance ("I'm in it for the money"); a mercenary. Stage 3: characters governed by shame and reputation — much of Jane Austen's social world; the people-pleaser terrified of disapproval. Stage 4: Inspector Javert, law-and-order as an absolute (and the tragedy of stage-4 rigidity when the law is unjust); Ned Stark's duty; a career soldier. Stage 5: Atticus Finch working *within* a flawed system while honoring rights above it; the constitutional reformer. Stage 6: Antigone burying her brother against Creon's law; Thoreau; Martin Luther King's "Letter from Birmingham Jail" (which explicitly invokes just-vs-unjust law); Sophie Scholl. A whole arc can *be* a stage climb — a Stage-2 rogue who grows into Stage-5 principle (Han Solo across a trilogy).

**What it gives you for character depth.** A ladder of *justification* — the ability to give two characters the same act with completely different moral engines behind it, and to write *principled conflict* between people at different stages (the Stage-4 officer and the Stage-6 dissident are both sincere, and that is the tragedy). It supplies a clean model of **moral growth as an arc**: cognitive conflict as the trigger, stage-climb as the shape. And it names the specific *content of an inner justification* the `{thought}` stream can render — a Stage-3 character literally thinks about who will approve.

**Combines-with.** The stages sit *above* Moral Foundations as reasoning-structures over intuitive contents (a Stage-4 and a Stage-6 person may both weight fairness heavily but *justify* it differently). Haidt's social-intuitionist model (below) is the great counter-argument — that this careful staged *reasoning* is mostly post-hoc — so hold the two in tension: Kohlberg describes the justification a character *offers*, Haidt warns it may not be the *cause*. Locus of control and the drives-schema orientation color which stage-reasoning a person reaches for under load. The stage a character *claims* vs the stage their behavior reveals is another `view: self` / truth split.

**Cautions.** (1) *Gender and culture bias* — Kohlberg's original sample was all boys; Gilligan (next) charged that the justice-orientation of the scale scored a care-orientation as lower, systematically. Cross-culturally, Stage 5–6 individualism scores higher than communitarian reasoning, arguably a Western bias. (2) *Judgment ≠ behavior* — people reason above how they act; a Stage-5 verbalizer can cheat like a Stage-2. (3) *Post-hoc rationalization* (the Haidt critique) — the interview may be measuring articulate justification, not the reasoning that drove the choice. (4) *Stage 6 is nearly empirical vapor* — so few people ever scored it that Kohlberg dropped it from the scoring manual; treat it as an ideal type. (5) Don't make a character a walking stage — the interest is in the *friction* between their claimed stage and their behavior.

**Sources.** Kohlberg, *Essays on Moral Development* (Vols. I–II, 1981/1984); Colby & Kohlberg, *The Measurement of Moral Judgment* (1987); Piaget, *The Moral Judgment of the Child* (1932); Rest et al., *Postconventional Moral Thinking* (1999, the neo-Kohlberg revision).

## Gilligan's ethics of care

**Name and lineage.** Carol **Gilligan**, *In a Different Voice* (1982) — a former collaborator of Kohlberg's who argued his justice-based scale mismeasured a whole moral orientation. The founding text of **care ethics** (developed further by Nel Noddings, *Caring*, 1984, and Virginia Held). Grew from Gilligan's studies of women facing real dilemmas (notably the abortion-decision study), not hypothetical ones.

**Core idea.** There are (at least) **two moral orientations**, not one immature-to-mature ladder: an **ethic of justice** (Kohlberg's — rights, fairness, impartial principle, the self as separate and autonomous) and an **ethic of care** (relationship, responsibility, responsiveness to particular others, the self as connected). The care voice reasons *contextually and narratively* ("who is connected to whom, and who will be hurt?") rather than by ranking abstract principles. Gilligan claimed this voice was more often (not exclusively) socialized into women and was wrongly scored as deficient by justice-centered scales. Care ethics also matures through stages — but the engine of growth is a changing **sense of self**, not rising cognitive capacity.

**Structure in full.** Three levels and two transitions:

- **Level 1 — Orientation to Individual Survival.** Care is directed at the *self*; the concern is one's own survival and needs. Pragmatic, self-protective.
  - *First transition — from selfishness to responsibility:* the recognition that one is *connected* to others and has responsibilities beyond the self.
- **Level 2 — Goodness as Self-Sacrifice.** Care is directed at *everyone but the self*; goodness is equated with sacrificing one's own needs for others, seeking approval by being selfless. The conventional feminine "good woman" — but built on a self-erasure that cannot last.
  - *Second transition — from goodness to truth:* the recognition that the self is *also* a person with legitimate needs, that self-sacrifice can be a form of dishonesty and even a hidden manipulation.
- **Level 3 — The Morality of Nonviolence.** Care is directed at *self and others held in a single web*; the governing principle becomes **do no harm — to anyone, including yourself.** Mature care balances one's own needs against others' within an ongoing web of relationship, refusing to sacrifice either pole.

**Vivid exemplars.** The self-sacrificing Level-2 mother who finally learns she "is a person too" is one of the most common women's arcs in fiction — from Nora in *A Doll's House* (walking out is the goodness-to-truth transition) to countless caretaker protagonists. Marmee and the March sisters negotiate care-and-self in *Little Women*. Level-3 care as a considered ethic: the hospice nurse, the character who refuses to abandon *either* their charge or themselves. Fanny Price stuck at Level 2 (self-effacement as virtue). The whole "I have to save everyone" hero whose arc is learning that *they* count too is Gilligan's Level 2 → 3 in genre dress.

**What it gives you for character depth.** A moral engine that is *relational and particular* rather than principled and abstract — the character who does not ask "what's the rule" but "who is in front of me and who will be hurt," which is how most real moral life actually feels and how many (not only women) characters should reason. It gives a distinct, common, and moving **arc** — self-erasing goodness → the hard truth that you count too → balanced care — that justice-ladder models miss entirely. And it names the *care-vs-justice clash* as a legitimate moral conflict between two good people, not a smart-vs-dumb one: the parent who breaks a rule to protect their child against the official who upholds it fairly.

**Combines-with.** This *is* the repo's care/harm foundation given a developmental spine (`docs/values-and-stakes.md`, Moral Foundations); it also loads heavily on HEXACO Agreeableness/Emotionality (Sentimentality, attachment) and the Panksepp **CARE** primary. The care-vs-justice split is a direct instance of the repo's "two good people who weight the menu differently → tragic, principled conflict" (`docs/values-and-stakes.md`). Gilligan's *self as connected vs separate* maps onto the SDT relatedness-vs-autonomy tension and the drives-schema's relatedness attachments. Her "changing sense of self drives the stages" is the Level-3 identity layer (`docs/character-model.md`) doing moral work.

**Cautions.** (1) *The gender claim is contested* — meta-analyses find the care/justice orientation difference by sex is small and heavily context-dependent; the *two-voices* distinction is robust and useful, the *women-reason-in-care* generalization is not. Use it as two orientations available to anyone, gendered only as a social tendency your world may or may not enforce. (2) *Romanticizing care* — care ethics can slide into valorizing self-sacrifice (exactly the Level-2 trap Gilligan diagnosed); the mature form *includes the self*. (3) Care without justice can be *partial* (favoring your own) — the flip side of justice without care being *cold*; the interesting characters feel the pull of both.

**Sources.** Gilligan, *In a Different Voice* (1982); Noddings, *Caring: A Feminine Approach to Ethics* (1984); Held, *The Ethics of Care* (2006); Jaffee & Hyde meta-analysis, "Gender differences in moral orientation" (*Psychological Bulletin*, 2000).

## Turiel's social domain theory (the moral/conventional distinction)

**Name and lineage.** Elliot **Turiel**, with Larry **Nucci** and Judith **Smetana** (1970s–1980s); *The Development of Social Knowledge: Morality and Convention* (1983). The major structural-developmental rival-and-complement to Kohlberg: where Kohlberg posited one moral dimension climbing through stages, Turiel argued that social knowledge is **partitioned into distinct domains from early childhood** — the moral is not a late achievement built out of convention, it is a separate track present young.

**Core idea.** People — even children of three — reliably sort social rules into three domains, judged by different criteria:
- **Moral** — acts that intrinsically affect others' welfare, rights, and fairness (hitting, stealing, cruelty). Judged **wrong independent of any rule or authority**, wrong *everywhere*, non-negotiable: "wrong even if the teacher says it's fine, wrong even in another country."
- **Conventional** — arbitrary, consensus-based uniformities that coordinate a group (dress, forms of address, table manners, ritual, titles). Wrong **only because** an authority or custom says so — **contingent, local, alterable**: fine if the rule changes or you go elsewhere.
- **Personal** — matters of private discretion over one's own body and life (hairstyle, friends, hobbies), outside moral *or* conventional regulation — the claimed zone of autonomy, and itself a site of struggle (adolescence is largely a renegotiation of where personal ends and parental convention begins).

The diagnostic is the **criterion-judgment battery**: is the act wrong if there is no rule against it? wrong in another country? could a legitimate authority make it OK? Moral violations come back rule-independent, universal, authority-independent; conventional ones the reverse.

**Structure / what it buys.** The developmental claim is **domains, not stages**: moral understanding (harm is wrong) is early and robust and does not wait on cognitive stage-climbing, while conventional understanding follows its own separate, zig-zagging course. The sharp tools are **domain placement** (which bin a given act falls in *for this person, in this culture*) and **domain mixing**: most consequential events are **multi-domain** (an act conventional to one party, moral to another) and cultures **moralize** conventions (demote or promote a rule across the line), which is where the drama lives.

**Vivid exemplars.** The rigid enforcer who **cannot tell a convention from a moral law** — who polices dress, address, and ritual with the fury owed to cruelty (the martinet; Dolores Umbridge moralizing decorum; the purity-obsessed authoritarian). Its inverse: the reformer who correctly **re-sees an "eternal law" as mere convention** and breaks it — every civil-disobedient who separates an unjust *statute* from *justice itself* (the discernment Antigone and King's "Letter from Birmingham Jail" have and Creon and Javert lack; note this is the same act Kohlberg scores as Stage 6 — Turiel says it is a *domain re-sort*, not a stage). Cross-cultural collision: one people's sanctity-convention (a food law, a veil, a form of bowing) read by another as a *moral* outrage — a great deal of tribal conflict is pure domain-confusion. The child who knows hitting is "wrong even if teacher allows it" but that talking-out-of-turn is "only wrong because of the rule."

**What it gives you for character depth.** A precise handle on **what a character holds sacred vs negotiable** — and the drama of getting it *wrong*: moralizing a convention (the tyranny of manners) or conventionalizing a moral (excusing cruelty as "just how it's done here"). It explains **principled rule-breaking without moral collapse** — the reformer has not lost their morals, they have correctly re-sorted a rule from the moral bin to the conventional one. And it gives **cross-cultural moral conflict** a clean, non-cartoon mechanism: not good-vs-evil but a domain boundary drawn in a different place.

**Combines-with.** The domains cut *across* Moral Foundations (`docs/values-and-stakes.md`): care/harm and fairness are prototypically *moral*-domain; loyalty, authority, and sanctity are the foundations most often carried in the *conventional* domain — and *moralized* to different degrees by different cultures, which is exactly the WEIRD-vs-traditional finding behind MFT. Turiel is the counterweight to Bandura (the moral domain is intuitively robust and hard to argue away — which is *why* disengagement has to attack it obliquely, by re-tagging the act's domain or the victim's status) and to naive relativism (the moral/conventional *distinction* recurs cross-culturally even as its *placement* varies). It sits beside Kohlberg as "domains, not stages" and refines Haidt's flash — is this intuition a welfare/harm read (moral domain) or a norm-violation read (conventional)?

**Cautions.** (1) *The boundary is culturally placed, not fixed* — Shweder's and Haidt's work shows much of morality lives outside a harm-based "moral" domain in non-WEIRD cultures (sanctity and authority are *moralized*, not merely conventional), so don't ship Turiel's harm-centric moral *content* as universal; treat the *distinction* as robust and its *placement* as a variable your world sets. (2) *Domain overlap is the norm* in rich situations — most interesting acts are multi-domain, so don't force a clean sort. (3) It is a theory of *judgment structure*, not behavior — knowing an act is moral-domain wrong doesn't make anyone do right (the Rest/Haidt gap applies).

**Sources.** Turiel, *The Development of Social Knowledge: Morality and Convention* (1983); Smetana, "Social-cognitive domain theory" (in Killen & Smetana, eds., *Handbook of Moral Development*, 2006); Nucci, *Education in the Moral Domain* (2001); Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "The Moral/Conventional Distinction."

## Rest's Four-Component Model and the neo-Kohlbergian DIT

**Name and lineage.** James **Rest** and the Minnesota group (1980s–1999); the neo-Kohlbergian reform of moral-development research, operationalized by the **Defining Issues Test (DIT / DIT-2)** — a multiple-choice successor to Kohlberg's interview that scores a "P-score" (preference for postconventional reasoning). Continued by Narvaez, Bebeau, Thoma.

**Core idea.** Moral judgment (Kohlberg's whole focus) is only *one* of **four distinct psychological processes** that must all fire for moral behavior to occur — and a person can be strong in one and fail in another, which is where most real moral failure lives. It replaces "stages" with three fuzzy, overlapping **schemas** of increasing sophistication.

**Structure in full.** The **Four Components** (each a separable capacity, each a possible point of failure):

1. **Moral Sensitivity** — *noticing* that a situation has a moral dimension at all, and reading who would be affected. Failure: the oblivious person who genuinely doesn't see the ethical stakes.
2. **Moral Judgment / Reasoning** — deciding which action is *right* (Kohlberg's territory). Failure: reasons badly.
3. **Moral Motivation / Focus** — *prioritizing* the moral value over competing ones (ambition, self-interest, comfort). Failure: knows the right thing, wants something else more.
4. **Moral Character / Implementation** — the ego-strength, courage, and follow-through to *actually do it* under pressure and distraction. Failure: knows, wants to, and still folds when it's hard.

The **three DIT schemas** (replacing the six stages):
- **Personal Interest schema** (≈ Kohlberg Stages 2–3) — what's good for me and mine.
- **Maintaining Norms schema** (≈ Stage 4) — law, order, established authority, uniform rules.
- **Postconventional schema** (≈ Stages 5–6) — shared ideals, appeals to principle, open to scrutiny.

**Vivid exemplars.** The Four Components are a *diagnostic for moral failure* that makes characters legible: the brilliant lawyer who reasons impeccably (Component 2) but is **motivationally** captured by ambition (fails 3) — every "sold out" arc. The good-hearted bystander who never **notices** the harm unfolding (fails 1) — the complacent citizen under a rising regime. The person who sees, judges, and *cares* but lacks the nerve to act (fails 4) — Peter's denial of Christ; the witness who won't testify. Conversely, a hero is someone in whom all four align under maximal pressure.

**What it gives you for character depth.** The best single tool here for **writing moral failure precisely** — instead of "he was weak," you can locate *which* of the four broke: didn't see it, saw-but-misjudged, judged-but-didn't-prioritize, or prioritized-but-couldn't-execute. Each is a different character and a different scene. It decouples *knowing the good* from *doing the good* — the gap Kohlberg's judgment-only focus hides and where most drama actually lives.

**Combines-with.** Component 4 ("moral character / implementation") is exactly the repo's *generated* courage — FEAR overridden by a stronger pull, resolved (`docs/generative-model.md`); a Component-4 failure is fear winning the collision. Component 3 (motivation/prioritizing) is the Model's value-weighting resolving a collision (`docs/values-and-stakes.md`, `docs/drives-schema.md`) — which stake wins. Component 1 (sensitivity) is the relevance gate: does the moral feature of the scene even get surfaced? Component 2 is Kohlberg/DIT reasoning. So the model tessellates cleanly onto the repo's own pipeline: perceive (1) → judge (2) → weight (3) → execute against fear (4).

**Cautions.** (1) The components are *analytically* separable but *interact* in real time (sensitivity shapes what you reason about); don't write them as a rigid pipeline. (2) DIT still inherits the "reasoning may be post-hoc" worry from Haidt. (3) The P-score, like all such measures, tracks articulate reasoning preference, not conduct.

**Sources.** Rest, *Moral Development: Advances in Research and Theory* (1986); Rest, Narvaez, Bebeau & Thoma, *Postconventional Moral Thinking: A Neo-Kohlbergian Approach* (1999); Narvaez & Rest, "The four components of acting morally" (1995).

## Haidt's social-intuitionist model

**Name and lineage.** Jonathan **Haidt**, "The Emotional Dog and Its Rational Tail" (*Psychological Review*, 2001); the empirical spearhead of the "intuitionist turn" in moral psychology (with Joshua Greene's dual-process neuroimaging). Precursor and companion to Moral Foundations Theory (the repo's ethics lens) and to *The Righteous Mind* (2012).

**Core idea.** Moral judgment is **not** primarily the product of reasoning. It is produced by fast, automatic, affect-laden **intuitions** — a flash of approval or disgust — and *then* reasoning is recruited, mostly **post-hoc**, to justify a verdict already reached. "The emotional dog wags its rational tail." Reasoning *can* sometimes override intuition, but far more often it is the press secretary, not the president. Because judgment is fast and social, we are chiefly persuaded not by arguments but by *other people's* intuitions (the "social" half). The clinching evidence is **moral dumbfounding** — people confidently condemn harmless taboo acts (consensual, private, no-one-hurt) and, when their every reason is dismantled, *keep the verdict and abandon the reasons* ("it's just wrong, I can't explain why").

**Structure in full.** The model is **six links** among four elements (person A's intuition, A's judgment, and the same for person B):

1. **The intuitive-judgment link** — intuition appears automatically and *causes* the judgment directly. (The dominant path.)
2. **The post-hoc reasoning link** — after judging, the person constructs a *justification*, searching for supporting reasons. (Does not cause the judgment; rationalizes it.)
3. **The reasoned-persuasion link** — the justification, voiced to *another* person, influences *their intuition* (persuasion works by triggering new intuitions, not by logic).
4. **The social-persuasion link** — merely *knowing* others' judgments shifts one's own intuition, with no argument at all (conformity, the felt pull of the group).
5. **The reasoned-judgment link** *(rare)* — sheer force of logic overrides intuition to reach a judgment against the gut. (Happens, but seldom, mostly in the philosophically trained.)
6. **The private-reflection link** *(rare)* — in reflection a person conjures a *new intuition* (e.g., by taking the other's perspective) that competes with and overturns the first. (The honest path to changing one's own mind — by re-feeling, not just re-thinking.)

Links 1–4 are the everyday engine; 5–6 are the exceptions rationalist models over-weight.

**Vivid exemplars.** Moral dumbfounding is a character goldmine: the person who *knows* their revulsion is irrational and clings to it anyway (Huck Finn's inverse — Huck's *gut* says help Jim while his *reasoning* says he's damned for it, and the gut wins: Haidt's model in a novel, with the intuition being the *good* one and the reasoning the corrupt social product). The zealot who generates fluent justifications for a conclusion fixed in advance (Link 2 in overdrive). The jury that convicts on a feeling and back-fills the reasons. The demagogue working Link 4 — shifting a crowd's judgment by sheer social contagion, no argument offered. The rare Link-6 character who genuinely changes by *imagining the other's position* until a new feeling forms (the essence of many redemption beats).

**What it gives you for character depth.** The corrective to writing characters as little judges who reason to verdicts: real people (and real characters) **feel first and justify after**, and the justification is often eloquent nonsense. This licenses — indeed demands — the gap between a character's stated reasons and their real drivers (the drives-schema `view: self` self-deception made a general law of moral cognition). It explains *moral stubbornness* (arguments don't move intuitions; only new intuitions, or social pressure, do) and gives the mechanism for the only two honest ways to change a mind on the page: re-feeling (Link 6) or social re-alignment (Link 4). It also warns the narrator: a character sincerely reporting *why* they did the moral thing is an unreliable narrator about their own morality.

**Combines-with.** Haidt's intuitions *are* Moral Foundations firing (the repo's ethics lens, `docs/values-and-stakes.md`) — the "taste-buds" deliver the flash; this model describes what happens *after* the flash. The intuition-first architecture is the **affect-as-information / somatic-marker** path in `emotion-and-decision` and the appraisal-spike of the Panksepp basis (`docs/generative-model.md`) — a moral judgment is an appraisal on the care/fairness/loyalty/etc. dimensions, felt before articulated. It is the great counterweight to Kohlberg/Rest above: hold "the character *reasons* to a verdict" (Kohlberg) against "the character *feels* a verdict and reasons afterward" (Haidt) and choose per character and per scene — the deliberate ethicist vs the gut-driven partisan are two real, different people.

**Cautions.** (1) *Not "reasoning never matters"* — Haidt allows Links 5–6; the strong claim is about *frequency and default*, not impossibility. Cool, low-stakes, or trained deliberation can and does reason to judgment. (2) The dumbfounding paradigm has been *partly challenged* (some "harmless" scenarios do carry perceived harm; effect sizes debated) — use it as a vivid tendency, not an iron law. (3) Overapplied, it makes every character a mere rationalizer, which is as flat as making them all Kantian judges; the interesting range is *how much* reason moves them and *when*.

**Sources.** Haidt, "The Emotional Dog and Its Rational Tail" (*Psychological Review*, 2001); Haidt, *The Righteous Mind* (2012); Haidt & Björklund, "Social intuitionists answer six questions about morality" (2008); Greene et al., "An fMRI investigation of emotional engagement in moral judgment" (*Science*, 2001).

## Bandura's moral disengagement

**Name and lineage.** Albert **Bandura** (Stanford), from social-cognitive theory; "Moral Disengagement in the Perpetration of Inhumanities" (1999) and *Moral Disengagement* (2016). The definitive account of how *ordinary people of ordinary conscience* commit and abet cruelty without feeling like monsters.

**Core idea.** People normally regulate their conduct against internalized moral standards — the anticipation of *self-sanction* (guilt, shame) deters wrongdoing. **Moral disengagement** is the set of psychosocial maneuvers that **selectively switch off** that self-sanction, letting a person do harm *while preserving their moral self-regard*. Crucially it is not a defect of character but a *normal cognitive capacity* everyone has and situations can activate — which is why decent people staff atrocities. The eight mechanisms operate at **four points** along the self-regulatory chain: reconstruing the **conduct**, obscuring the **agency**, minimizing the **consequences**, and devaluing the **victim**.

**Structure in full.** Eight mechanisms, by locus:

- **Reconstruing the conduct** (making the act itself seem acceptable):
  1. **Moral Justification** — the harm serves a worthy cause; violence becomes righteous ("killing to protect the faith / freedom / family"). The most powerful and dangerous, because it makes cruelty a *duty*.
  2. **Euphemistic Labeling** — sanitizing language drains the act of its reality ("collateral damage," "enhanced interrogation," "letting someone go" for firing, "wet work").
  3. **Advantageous Comparison** — contrast with something worse makes the act look benign ("a few casualties versus the millions the enemy would kill").
- **Obscuring or displacing agency** (making oneself not-the-author):
  4. **Displacement of Responsibility** — "I was only following orders"; an authority is the real agent (the Milgram engine).
  5. **Diffusion of Responsibility** — the harm is spread across a group, a division of labor, a committee, a mob; no one owns it ("everyone was doing it," "I just filed the paperwork").
- **Minimizing the consequences**:
  6. **Distortion / Disregard of Consequences** — ignoring, discounting, or disbelieving the harm done; not looking; the drone operator who never sees the ground.
- **Devaluing the target**:
  7. **Dehumanization** — stripping the victim of human qualities (vermin, cockroaches, animals, numbers) so that harming them stops registering as harming a person. The prerequisite of genocide.
  8. **Attribution of Blame** — the victim brought it on themselves; they provoked it, deserved it, asked for it ("she shouldn't have been there," "they started it").

**Vivid exemplars.** The whole taxonomy is a villain-and-complicity engine. Moral justification: the Inquisitor, the terrorist convinced of the cause, Walter White's "I did it for my family." Euphemism: the corporate/bureaucratic language of *Catch-22* and every atrocity memo. Displacement: Eichmann's "I was following orders" (Arendt's "banality of evil" is disengagement theory avant la lettre). Diffusion: the firing squad with one blank rifle so no one knows who killed; the committee that greenlights a disaster. Dehumanization: Orwell's *1984*, the Ministry propaganda; wartime racial epithets. Blame-attribution: the abuser's "look what you made me do." A single character can *escalate through* the mechanisms across an arc — a good soldier who starts at displacement ("orders") and, to keep functioning, slides into dehumanization — which is exactly how the model says corruption *deepens*.

**What it gives you for character depth.** The answer to the single hardest problem in writing evil: **how does a person who thinks of themselves as good do monstrous things and still sleep?** Not by being a cackling monster (rare and flat) but by *selectively disengaging* — and the eight named mechanisms give you eight precise, realistic scripts for it. It makes complicity legible (the bureaucrat, the bystander, the "just doing my job") and gives *interiority to a villain* — the `{thought}` stream of a disengaged perpetrator is not "I am evil" but "this is necessary / they aren't really people / it's not my call." It also models moral *slide*: disengagement is progressive; each use lowers the bar for the next.

**Combines-with.** Disengagement is the mechanism by which the repo's severity computation gets *suppressed*: an act that structurally violates care/harm (`docs/values-and-stakes.md`) has its felt severity throttled before it reaches self-sanction — dehumanization literally zeroes the victim's weight, moral justification re-tags the harm as service to a higher value. In generative terms it is how CARE is *decoupled* so RAGE or SEEKING runs unchecked (`docs/generative-model.md` — "cruelty = RAGE unchecked by CARE"): the mechanisms are the specific ways the check is removed. It pairs with low **Honesty-Humility** and the Dark-Tetrad overlay (`docs/trait-theory.md`) — but note the sharp, useful difference: the psychopath *has no* self-sanction to disengage; moral disengagement is the machinery by which the *normal-conscience* person harms, which is the more common and more disturbing case. It is also the villain-side complement to Rest's Component-4 failure and Haidt's Link-2 (post-hoc justification weaponized).

**Cautions.** (1) *Not exculpatory* — the theory explains, it does not excuse; a character who disengages is still culpable, and stories that use it as a get-out-of-guilt card ring false. (2) *Everyone does it a little* — mild disengagement (the small euphemism, the diffused responsibility of a group) is universal; reserve the heavy mechanisms (dehumanization) for genuine atrocity or they lose force. (3) The most chilling use is *not* the villain but the *ordinary* character you've made sympathetic sliding into it — resist making disengagement a mark of the Other. (4) Overlaps with rationalization generally; keep the *self-sanction-switching-off* specificity — the point is a person maintaining moral self-regard *while* harming.

**Sources.** Bandura, "Moral Disengagement in the Perpetration of Inhumanities" (*Personality and Social Psychology Review*, 1999); Bandura, *Moral Disengagement: How People Do Harm and Live with Themselves* (2016); Bandura, Barbaranelli, Caprara & Pastorelli, "Mechanisms of moral disengagement" (*JPSP*, 1996); Hannah Arendt, *Eichmann in Jerusalem* (1963, the parallel humanistic account).

## The moral emotions: shame, guilt, and the self-conscious family

**Name and lineage.** Helen Block **Lewis** (*Shame and Guilt in Neurosis*, 1971) drew the modern shame/guilt distinction; June Price **Tangney** operationalized and validated it (the **Test of Self-Conscious Affect, TOSCA**, from 1989; *Shame and Guilt*, with Dearing, 2002). Jonathan **Haidt** mapped the wider family ("The Moral Emotions," 2003); Paul **Rozin**, Laura Lowery, Sumio Imada & Haidt gave the **CAD triad** (1999). This is the affective complement to every *reasoning* model in this file — the emotions that *are* the moral reaction, before and beneath the justification.

**Core idea.** Moral life runs on specific emotions that appraise self and others against moral standards and carry **action tendencies** — they don't merely color a judgment, they *push* a behavior. The load-bearing distinction is **shame vs guilt**, two self-blaming emotions with **opposite** dynamics:
- **Guilt** = a negative evaluation of a *specific act* ("I did a bad **thing**"). Focus on the controllable behavior and its effect on the wronged; the self stays intact. Action tendency: **reparation** — confess, apologize, undo, approach. Correlates with empathy and pro-social repair.
- **Shame** = a negative evaluation of the *whole self* ("I **am** a bad, worthless person"). Focus on a global, stable, exposed self. Action tendency: **hide, shrink, escape** — and, because a globally bad self is unbearable, to **externalize blame** and lash outward. Correlates, counter-intuitively, with *more* anger, aggression, and relapse — not less.

So the *same* transgression breeds redemption or ruin depending on which of the two it triggers — the single most useful moral-emotion fact for character.

**Structure in full — the moral-emotion families (Haidt).**
- **Self-conscious** (judging the self): **guilt, shame, embarrassment,** and **pride** — with the pride mirror of the shame/guilt split: *hubristic* pride ("I am great," global self) vs *authentic* pride ("I did well," specific act).
- **Other-condemning** (judging others' violations): **contempt, anger, disgust** — Rozin's **CAD triad**, each tuned to a class of violation: **C**ontempt ↔ violations of *community* (hierarchy, duty); **A**nger ↔ violations of *autonomy* (rights, harm, fairness); **D**isgust ↔ violations of *divinity* (purity, sanctity) — mapping onto Shweder's "big three" ethics and prefiguring Moral Foundations.
- **Other-suffering** (feeling another's pain): **compassion / sympathy** — the CARE-driven approach emotion.
- **Other-praising** (uplift at others' goodness): **gratitude**, **awe**, and **elevation** — Haidt's warm, expanding response to witnessing moral beauty, which motivates the witness to *become* better (the emotional engine under many inspiration and redemption beats).

**Vivid exemplars.** The shame/guilt fork is a whole-arc generator: **Jean Valjean** (guilt → a lifetime of reparation and integration) against **Javert** (shame — being *wrong*, the just-self he built exposed as false, is literally unsurvivable; he cannot repair, so he dies). **Raskolnikov**'s arc *is* the migration from shame-defiance ("I am an extraordinary man, above the law") to guilt-confession-and-repair — Dostoevsky's whole engine. **Dimmesdale**'s concealed shame rots him from inside (hiding, the shame script) while **Hester** wears the mark in public and turns it to good (nearer the guilt/reparation path). Shame's outward lash: the humiliated man who turns violent, the exposed bully — shame → rage → aggression, the counter-intuitive spiral. **Elevation** as a beat: the cynic who witnesses a stranger's unbought kindness and is quietly remade. Contempt and disgust as an *othering* engine feeding straight into Bandura's dehumanization — disgust is the purity emotion that most easily strips a person of humanity.

**What it gives you for character depth.** The **affective layer the moral-reasoning models skip** — most people don't reason to a verdict, they *feel* guilt or shame or disgust and move. The shame/guilt distinction yields two utterly different characters from the *same* wrongdoing and forecasts their arcs (repair vs conceal-and-destroy); it is the mechanism behind whether a fall becomes a redemption or a ruin, and whether a character can *cross* from one to the other. **Guilt-proneness** is the very disposition the meta-section cites (Cohen et al.) as a robust predictor of *not* transgressing. The CAD triad tells you *which* emotion a given violation fires, and therefore how a witness moves (contempt withdraws, anger attacks, disgust expels). And **elevation** names the rare positive moral emotion that pulls a character *toward* the good.

**Combines-with.** These emotions are the **self-sanction** Bandura's disengagement switches off — anticipated guilt and shame are exactly what moral justification and dehumanization suppress — so this section and Bandura's are two faces of one system: the self-conscious emotions are the guard, disengagement is how the guard is bypassed. They are **appraisals on the Panksepp basis** (`docs/generative-model.md`): guilt and shame are PANIC/GRIEF-toned self-appraisals, moral anger is RAGE tagged to a fairness/harm violation, compassion is CARE, disgust its own system in some accounts — the moral emotion *is* a primary spiked by a moral appraisal, felt before named (Haidt's intuition given its specific affective identity). Guilt-proneness anchors to **Honesty-Humility** and low Dark-Tetrad (`docs/trait-theory.md`); a **shame-prone** disposition is a distinct, darker temperament worth authoring on a principal (its tie to withdrawal, externalized blame, and aggression is a ready wound-and-defense in drives-schema terms — `docs/drives-schema.md`). CAD ↔ Moral Foundations ↔ Shweder's three ethics are one mapping from three angles (`docs/values-and-stakes.md`).

**Cautions.** (1) *Shame is not just "stronger guilt"* — the whole point is the **self-vs-act** focus and their opposite action tendencies; collapse them and the mechanism is gone. (2) *The TOSCA measures are contested* — critics argue the scenarios confound the emotion with its behavioral response; treat shame-/guilt-proneness as a robust and useful *tendency*, not a settled quantity. (3) *Shame is culturally central* — honor/face "shame cultures" (`worldbuilding-frameworks`) build whole moral orders on it, where the Western clinical read of shame-as-pathology misleads; set shame's valence by world. (4) Don't reflexively make every wrongdoer guilt-prone-and-redeemable *or* shame-locked-and-doomed — the interest is *which*, and whether they can migrate (Raskolnikov's crossing is the arc).

**Sources.** Tangney & Dearing, *Shame and Guilt* (2002); Lewis, *Shame and Guilt in Neurosis* (1971); Tangney, Stuewig & Mashek, "Moral emotions and moral behavior" (*Annual Review of Psychology*, 2007); Haidt, "The Moral Emotions" (in Davidson, Scherer & Goldsmith, eds., *Handbook of Affective Sciences*, 2003); Rozin, Lowery, Imada & Haidt, "The CAD triad hypothesis" (*JPSP*, 1999).

## The scholarly models of moral character: trait vs social-cognitive vs virtue

**Name and lineage.** Not one theory but the *meta-debate* over what moral character even *is* — the through-line from **Hartshorne & May's** *Studies in the Nature of Character* (1928–30, the first big empirical crack) through the **person–situation debate** (Walter Mischel, *Personality and Assessment*, 1968) through **situationism** in philosophy (Gilbert Harman 1999; John **Doris**, *Lack of Character*, 2002) to the reconciling **social-cognitive** and **moral-identity** models (Bandura; Mischel & Shoda's CAPS, 1995; Blasi 1980; Aquino & Reed 2002) and the recent **trait** revival in organizational psychology (Cohen, Panter, Morse). Three answers to "where does moral behavior come from?"

**Core idea.** There are three live construals of moral character, and the interesting truth is their *interaction*:

- **The trait construal** — moral character is a set of *broad, stable dispositions* (honesty, compassion, integrity) that a person carries across situations; the Aristotelian/VIA/HEXACO-Honesty-Humility view. Prediction: character is consistent; the honest person is honest everywhere.
- **The social-cognitive construal** — moral character is a *system of context-sensitive processes* (accessible self-schemas, if-then behavioral signatures, construals of the situation) rather than a fixed quantity; behavior is a *person × situation* interaction. Prediction: character is *coherent* (patterned, `if X then honest, if Y then not`) but not globally *consistent*.
- **The virtue/practice construal** — moral character is neither a static trait nor a mere reaction pattern but an achieved *practical excellence* (phronesis-guided virtue), developed by habituation and expressed as skilled perception-and-action. Prediction: the fully virtuous are reliable *because* practically wise, but they are rare and most people are a patchwork.

**Structure in full.** The key positions and constructs:

- **The person–situation debate (Mischel, 1968).** Cross-situational behavioral *consistency* correlations rarely exceed ~0.30 (the "personality coefficient"); knowing a trait score predicts behavior in a single situation weakly. First read as "traits are a myth"; later resolved by *aggregation* (traits predict *averaged* behavior well) and by CAPS.
- **CAPS — the Cognitive-Affective Personality System (Mischel & Shoda, 1995).** Personality is a stable network of cognitions and affects (encodings, expectancies, goals, self-regulatory plans) that produces *if-then situation-behavior signatures*. A person isn't "aggressive" flatly; they are "aggressive-if-humiliated-by-a-peer, warm-if-approached-by-a-child." **Stability lives in the *pattern*, not the average.** This is the repo's whole-trait theory in social-cognitive dress — a mean *plus* the situation-contingent variability.
- **Situationism (Harman 1999; Doris 2002).** Marshals the classic social-psych experiments — beginning decades earlier with **Hartshorne & May's** *Studies in the Nature of Character* (1928–30), the mammoth **Character Education Inquiry**: thousands of schoolchildren given chances to lie, cheat, and steal across many separate settings, finding honesty was **not a unified trait** — a child who cheated on one task was barely more likely to cheat on another, so behavior tracked the *situation*, not a global "honesty" (their **doctrine of specificity**, the founding empirical shot in this whole debate; note later factor-reanalyses recovered *some* underlying generality, so it overstated) — then Milgram (obedience), Darley & Batson's **Good Samaritan** study (seminarians in a hurry step over a groaning man; those with time help — situational press swamps "compassion"), Isen & Levin's dime-in-the-phone-booth (finding a coin makes you help), the Stanford Prison Experiment — to argue that **broad, cross-situational moral character traits do not empirically exist**; behavior is driven by trivial situational features, not global virtue. Radical conclusion: virtue ethics rests on an empirical illusion. Moderate conclusion (Doris): keep only *local, narrow* traits ("honest-when-not-time-pressured-and-alone").
- **The reconciliation.** Most researchers now hold a *person × situation interactionism*: broad traits are real and predict aggregated conduct, *and* situations powerfully move single acts, *and* the stable unit may be the if-then signature. Character is coherent, not globally consistent.
- **Moral identity (Blasi's self-model, 1980; Aquino & Reed, 2002).** The bridge from judgment to action: what makes a person *act* on a moral judgment is how central morality is to their **self-concept** — their **moral identity**. Aquino & Reed's *Self-Importance of Moral Identity* has two dimensions: **Internalization** (how central moral traits are to one's private sense of self) and **Symbolization** (how much one expresses them publicly). A situational cue that *activates* (primes) moral identity in the working self-concept raises the motivation to act morally; a cue that activates a rival identity (professional, tribal) can crowd it out. This is Rest's Component 3 (motivation) mechanized: the moral self must be *accessible* at the moment of choice.
- **The trait revival (Cohen, Panter, Morse, 2014).** "Guilt-proneness" and related dispositions *do* predict unethical behavior robustly across contexts — a defense of broad moral traits after the situationist assault, and the empirical backbone for treating Honesty-Humility as a real, predictive dimension.

**Vivid exemplars.** Situationism on the page: the decent person who does the terrible thing because the *situation* engineered it — every "I never thought I was capable of this" arc, the ordinary reservist at Abu Ghraib, the seminarian late for his sermon on the Good Samaritan who steps over the victim (Doris's favorite, and a devastating scene in itself). CAPS if-then coherence: the war hero who is a coward at home, the tyrant-boss who is tender with his dog — not hypocrisy but *signature*, and far realer than a flat "brave man." Moral identity as the deciding variable: two people reach the same verdict that they *should* whistle-blow; the one for whom "honest person" is identity-central does it, the one for whom it's peripheral doesn't (Internalization deciding the act). A character *losing* moral-identity accessibility under a rival identity (the good accountant whose "loyal employee" self is primed until the fraud feels normal) is disengagement and identity-crowding in one.

**What it gives you for character depth.** This is the *governing frame* for using every other framework in this file honestly. It tells you **not to write global saints and global monsters** — real moral character is *coherent but situationally patterned* (CAPS if-then signatures), which is precisely the repo's whole-trait tails and cross-layer opposition (a virtue that holds in one situation and breaks in another is not a contradiction, it's a signature). It supplies the **situational lever** that lets you move a character by *circumstance* rather than force (the whole director's discipline, `dramatic-structure`): change the situation and the "honest" person lies, believably. And **moral identity** gives the missing variable between judgment and deed — *how central being-moral is to this self* — which decides who acts and who folds when the verdict is clear.

**Combines-with.** CAPS *is* whole-trait theory (`docs/trait-theory.md`) — the (mean, variability) distribution and its situation-contingent sampling; the if-then signature is the "out-of-character tail, produced not random" made systematic. Moral identity is the Level-3 self-image / identity layer (`docs/character-model.md`, `docs/drives-schema.md` self-image goals) doing the deciding — "someone who never abandons the sick" *is* an internalized moral identity, and its *accessibility* at decision time is a relevance-gate question. Situationism is the empirical warrant for the repo's central move: **directing by circumstance without the seam showing** (a deep world can plausibly supply the situational press that bends the character, `docs/generative-model.md`). Guilt-proneness and Honesty-Humility are the broad-trait anchors (`docs/trait-theory.md`). Rest's four components, Haidt's intuitions, and Bandura's disengagement all slot in as the *processes* the social-cognitive system runs.

**Cautions.** (1) *Don't over-learn situationism* — the "traits don't exist" conclusion is the radical, minority reading; the mainstream is interactionism, and broad traits *do* predict aggregated conduct. Writing every character as pure situational putty is as false as writing them as fixed virtue-statues. (2) The famous experiments are *contested* — the Stanford Prison Experiment is now widely regarded as methodologically compromised and partly staged; Milgram's numbers are real but the interpretation is debated; cite them as *illustrations of a tendency*, not settled proof. (3) *Coherence is not consistency* — the payoff is patterned variability (if-then), not randomness; a character who is unpredictable in *every* situation isn't deep, just noisy. (4) Moral identity can be *self-deceived* — a person whose symbolization (public display) outruns their internalization is the sanctimonious hypocrite, a rich `view: self` gap.

**Sources.** Hartshorne & May, *Studies in the Nature of Character, Vol. I: Studies in Deceit* (1928); Doris, *Lack of Character: Personality and Moral Behavior* (2002); Mischel & Shoda, "A cognitive-affective system theory of personality" (*Psychological Review*, 1995); Aquino & Reed, "The self-importance of moral identity" (*JPSP*, 2002); Blasi, "Bridging moral cognition and moral action" (*Psychological Bulletin*, 1980); Cohen, Panter et al., "Moral character in the workplace" (*JPSP*, 2014); Harman, "Moral philosophy meets social psychology: virtue ethics and the fundamental attribution error" (1999).

## Adjacent vice and virtue taxonomies (a short well)

Not full framework sections, but essential companions for the *shadow* side and for non-Western breadth — reach for these when a character needs a named vice or a moral tradition other than the Greco-liberal default.

- **The cardinal and theological virtues (Plato/Stoics → Aquinas).** The four **cardinal virtues** — **Prudence** (practical wisdom), **Justice**, **Fortitude** (courage), **Temperance** — plus the three **theological virtues** of Christian tradition — **Faith, Hope, Charity (Love/*caritas*)**. The backbone of Western moral vocabulary for two millennia; period and religious characters *think* in these terms. Aquinas's *Summa* is the systematic treatment.
- **The seven deadly sins and their remedial virtues.** The great **vice taxonomy** the VIA lacks: **Pride** (vs Humility), **Greed/Avarice** (vs Charity), **Wrath** (vs Patience), **Envy** (vs Kindness/Gratitude), **Lust** (vs Chastity), **Gluttony** (vs Temperance), **Sloth/*acedia*** (vs Diligence). Evagrius's eight *logismoi* → Gregory the Great's seven (590 CE) → Dante's *Purgatorio* (which structures a whole moral cosmos by them). Each sin is a *disordered love* — a good desire aimed wrong or to excess (Dante's schema: love perverted, deficient, or excessive) — which makes them generative rather than moralistic: every sin is a virtue's raw material misdirected. Unbeatable for giving a character a *defining flaw* with a built-in arc (the sin and its remedy).
- **Confucian virtue ethics (the *Analects*, c. 500 BCE; Mencius; Xunzi).** The great non-Western virtue tradition. Core virtues: **Ren** (仁, humaneness/benevolence — the supreme virtue, fellow-feeling), **Li** (礼, ritual propriety — the forms of respectful conduct that shape character), **Yi** (义, righteousness/rightness), **Zhi** (智, wisdom), **Xin** (信, trustworthiness), and **Xiao** (孝, filial piety — the root of *ren*). The ideal is the **junzi** (君子, the "exemplary person / gentleman"), against the **xiaoren** (petty person). Character is cultivated relationally and through *li* (practiced ritual forms *make* the inner virtue — habituation, like Aristotle's, but socially embedded). Indispensable for building any character in a Sinic or Confucian-influenced world, and a genuine alternative moral *architecture* — role-and-relationship-based rather than rule- or rights-based, closer to Gilligan's care web than to Kant.
- **Nietzschean master/slave morality (*Beyond Good and Evil*, *On the Genealogy of Morals*, 1886–87).** Not a virtue list but a *critique* and a source of anti-heroic moral psychology: **master morality** (values springing from strength, self-affirmation — "good/bad" as noble/contemptible) vs **slave morality** (values born of *ressentiment*, the weak revaluing their powerlessness as virtue — "good/evil," where the powerful are recast as evil). Essential for villains who *reject* the conventional moral frame on principle, for Übermensch-flavored characters, and for the psychology of *ressentiment* (envy transfigured into righteousness) — a specific, potent engine for a certain kind of antagonist or corrupted idealist.

**Combines-with.** The seven sins map onto Panksepp primaries and low-pole traits (Wrath = RAGE/low-Agreeableness; Sloth = low SEEKING/low-C; Greed/Pride = low Honesty-Humility) and give the *vice* names the VIA strengths' overuse produces. The cardinal/theological virtues overlap heavily with VIA and Aristotle (Prudence = phronesis; Fortitude = courage). Confucian *ren/xiao* is care/loyalty (Moral Foundations) built into a role structure; *li* is habituation. Nietzsche's *ressentiment* is envy (a deadly sin) + moral-disengagement-in-reverse (revaluing to license one's own resentment). Use these for period fidelity, non-Western worlds, and named flaws with arcs.

**Sources.** Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* (IIa-IIae, on the virtues and vices); Dante, *Purgatorio*; Confucius, *Analects* (trans. Slingerland); Mencius; Nietzsche, *On the Genealogy of Morals* (1887); Bryan Van Norden, *Introduction to Classical Chinese Philosophy* (2011).

## How to reach into this file

Start from the repo defaults and treat this file as the reasons behind them and the range around them. When you need to **know what a person will and won't do under pressure**, remember first that the repo *generates* the virtue rather than storing it — courage is FEAR overridden, resolved (`docs/generative-model.md`) — and use these frameworks to *name and justify* that resolution, never to add a "morality" field. Reach for **Aristotle's mean** when you want a virtue drawn with its two flanking vices (the surest way to make a good trait dramatic); for **VIA** when you need a positive vocabulary of what a character is good at *being* (signature strengths → a likable protagonist fast); for **Kohlberg/Gilligan/Rest** when you're charting a moral *arc* or writing principled conflict between two sincere people (justice-voice vs care-voice; Stage-4 duty vs Stage-6 conscience; which of the four components breaks); for **Turiel's moral/conventional distinction** when a character treats a mere convention as sacred, or a reformer correctly re-sorts an "eternal law" into the negotiable bin, or two cultures collide over where the line falls; for the **moral emotions (shame vs guilt)** when a single wrongdoing must fork toward redemption (guilt → reparation) or ruin (shame → conceal, externalize, lash out) — the fastest lever on whether a fall is recoverable; for **Haidt** whenever a character's stated reasons should be distrusted (feel-first, justify-after — the moral case of the drives-schema `view: self`); for **Bandura's disengagement** whenever an ordinary-conscience person must do or abet harm and keep their self-regard (eight named scripts for the villain and, more chillingly, the sympathetic character sliding); and for the **trait-vs-social-cognitive-vs-virtue meta-debate** as the governing discipline over all of them — write moral character as *coherent but situationally patterned* (CAPS if-then signatures = the whole-trait tails), let **moral identity** decide who acts on a verdict, and use **situationism** as the license to bend a character by circumstance rather than force. For the shadow and for non-default worlds, the closing well: the seven deadly sins for named flaws-with-arcs, Confucian *ren/li/junzi* for a role-based moral architecture, Nietzschean *ressentiment* for the antagonist who rejects the frame. Every framework here is an observer's theory of the pattern; the pattern itself is produced by the generator, weighted by the Model, and rendered as the cost in the `{thought}` stream.
