# Voice, Cognition and Surface

The observable person and the style of the mind that drives them — **Layer 8 (SURFACE)** and the cognitive slice of **Layer 4 (MIND)** in `character-anatomy.md`. Everything else in this toolbox is interior (dispositions, values, drives, the weighting model); this file is the thin skin the reader actually touches. A stranger does not read a character's HEXACO profile — they hear a sentence, watch a hand, catch a tell, and infer the rest. That inference is the whole social engine: `self-and-perception.md` builds the *others-see* view by **thin-slicing observable actions**, and the narrator (`narration.md`) can only render what the POV *witnesses* — so the surface is where the simulation becomes legible to other characters and to the reader alike. This file is the well of frameworks for that layer: how speech carries identity (register, idiolect, function words, the pragmatics of a line, the way a person occupies the floor), how the body leaks state (the nonverbal channels, the face, the honest limits of tell-reading), how a mind has a recognisable *style* (cognitive styles, locus and explanatory style, biases-as-quirks), and how observers turn a two-second slice into a whole-person judgement (thin-slicing, the lens model). The governing craft question throughout: **what makes a character recognisable in a single line** — and the governing discipline: the surface is a *sample of the interior* (`generative-model.md`), never an authored costume. A verbal tic that traces to nothing is a party trick; a verbal tic that is the audible shape of a fear is character. The repo's commitments — HEXACO traits, Schwartz/Moral-Foundations worth, the Panksepp 7-primary affective basis, the drives schema (which already owns **locus of control** as a Layer-3 orientation) — are the reference defaults these surfaces express *through*. Store the interior; author the surface as its readout; let the observer supply the label.

## Register and idiolect — the sociolinguistic layer of voice

**Name and lineage.** The sociolinguistic tradition: **Martin Joos**, *The Five Clocks* (1961), on stylistic register; **M.A.K. Halliday**'s systemic-functional account of register (field/tenor/mode, 1970s); **Basil Bernstein**'s restricted vs elaborated codes (1960s–70s); **William Labov**'s variationist sociolinguistics (the department-store study, 1966; *Sociolinguistic Patterns*, 1972), which showed pronunciation varies systematically with class, context, and attention to speech. The umbrella term **idiolect** (an individual's unique language system) descends from structural linguistics (Bloch, 1948).

**Core idea.** No one speaks one way. Every person commands a *repertoire* of varieties and shifts among them by situation, audience, and self-presentation. The variety is not decoration — it *encodes* who the speaker is (dialect, class, region, generation, trade) and *who they take the listener to be* (register, formality, distance). An idiolect is the individual fingerprint that survives across all of a person's registers; register is the dial they turn within it. A character's voice is an idiolect plus a *characteristic register range* plus the *tells of when and why they shift*.

**Structure in full.**

*Joos's five registers ("clocks"), by formality:*
- **Frozen** — fixed, unalterable, ritual text: vows, the Pledge, liturgy, legal boilerplate. Recited, not composed. A character who speaks in frozen register off-ritual (quotes scripture in a bar fight) marks zealotry or armour.
- **Formal** — one-way, structured, complete-sentence, no interruption expected: the lecture, the courtroom, the eulogy. Precise, impersonal, low redundancy, no slang.
- **Consultative** — the default of professional strangers cooperating: two-way, background supplied, listener participates with backchannels ("mm-hm", "right"). The register of most work.
- **Casual** — peers and friends: ellipsis, slang, in-group reference, interruption welcome. Shared background *assumed*, so much goes unsaid.
- **Intimate** — private dyads: near-absent grammar, private vocabulary, half-words, pet names, the sentence the other finishes. The most compressed register; an outsider can barely parse it.

*Halliday's three register variables (what fixes the register in a situation):*
- **Field** — what is being talked about, the domain and its jargon (a surgeon's field-vocabulary).
- **Tenor** — the relationship and status between speakers (deferential vs peer vs commanding).
- **Mode** — the channel and its rhetoric (spoken vs written, monologue vs dialogue, spontaneous vs planned).

*Bernstein's two codes:*
- **Restricted code** — condensed, context-dependent, high-solidarity speech among people who share so much that they needn't spell it out ("Put it over there, you know, the usual"). Group-bonding, implicit.
- **Elaborated code** — explicit, context-independent, spelled-out speech for audiences who *don't* share the background. (Bernstein tied these to class-linked access, a claim now read cautiously — but as a *stylistic* contrast it is invaluable.)

*Idiolect components (the individual fingerprint):* characteristic vocabulary and pet words; syntactic habits (long periodic sentences vs clipped fragments); favoured metaphor domains; discourse markers ("look", "so", "I mean", "right?"); pronunciation/accent; and the *register range* a person can or will reach (some people have no casual register; some cannot do formal).

*Code-switching* — moving between languages, dialects, or registers, often mid-utterance, to signal identity, solidarity, distance, or exclusion. *Style-shifting* (Labov) is the intra-dialect version: the same speaker's care with pronunciation rises with formality and self-monitoring.

**Vivid exemplars.** Eliza Doolittle *is* this framework — the plot is a forced march from one sociolect to another, and the drama is the seams. Tyrion Lannister slides register with lethal control — courtly-formal to a queen, gutter-casual in a brothel, and the switch itself is a weapon. Stringer Bell (*The Wire*) code-switches between the corner and the boardroom and is destroyed in the gap between them. Hagrid's eye-dialect ("Yeh're a wizard, Harry") fixes class and warmth in one line. Gollum's private idiolect ("my precious", "we hates it") is a whole fractured self rendered as grammar. A character who says "one doesn't" instead of "you don't" has been placed on the map before the scene starts.

**What it gives you for character depth.** The fastest, cheapest characterisation channel: register places a person on every axis of `character-anatomy.md` Layer 9 (position — class, era, region, trade) in a single sentence, and the *shift* reveals Layer 5 (relationships — tenor is literally the relationship) and Layer 7 (state — register collapses toward casual/intimate under stress or drink, and stiffens toward formal under threat or performance). The idiolect is the through-line that keeps a voice recognisable across scenes; the register range is a character knob (the man with no intimate register; the woman who goes formal precisely when she's most hurt).

**Combines-with.** Register-shift is the audible face of the **Interpersonal Circumplex** tenor (`trait-models.md`): a move toward dominance stiffens register, a move toward warmth softens it. Idiolect vocabulary samples **Openness** and **position**; the *fluency and control* of shifting samples social intelligence and **eXtraversion**. Casual/intimate collapse under high arousal ties to the **state engine** (`state-engine.md`) and Easterbrook cue-narrowing (`emotion-and-decision`). The *content* a register carries is filtered by the **vault** (`knowledge-model.md`) — a character can only deploy jargon they know. And register is the tenor input to **politeness** (below).

**Cautions.** (1) *Eye-dialect is a trap* — spelling out accent phonetically ("wot", "guv'nor") reads as condescension and clogs the page; suggest dialect through *syntax, rhythm, and word-choice*, not misspelling (the reality-effect discipline, `narrative-craft`). (2) *Register must be consistent within a self* — a character's idiolect is the constraint; drift that isn't *caused* (by state, audience, arc) reads as the author losing the voice. (3) Bernstein's code/class link is contested and was weaponised as a deficit theory — use the elaborated/restricted *contrast* as stylistics, drop the class determinism. (4) One dialect ≠ one personality — do not let a regional accent *become* the character (the cliché-trap of the folksy-wise rustic).

**Sources.** Joos, *The Five Clocks* (1961); Halliday, *Language as Social Semiotic* (1978); Bernstein, *Class, Codes and Control* (1971); Labov, *Sociolinguistic Patterns* (1972); Biber & Conrad, *Register, Genre, and Style* (2009).

## Function words — the psychological fingerprint of speech

**Name and lineage.** **James W. Pennebaker** and colleagues (Texas), from the mid-1990s: the **LIWC** (Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count) program and the research summarised in *The Secret Life of Pronouns* (2011). A quantitative, computational wing of the sociolinguistic story.

**Core idea.** The words that carry a sentence's *meaning* — nouns, verbs — are consciously chosen and reveal *what* someone is talking about. The tiny **function words** — pronouns, articles, prepositions, conjunctions, auxiliary verbs, quantifiers — are produced almost entirely below awareness, at high frequency, and it is *these* that betray *how a person relates to what they're saying*: their status, honesty, focus, emotional state, and even their sex, age, and mental health. Function words are a "psychic fingerprint" precisely because they are too small and frequent to fake.

**Structure in full.** The load-bearing markers (each an observable dial):
- **First-person singular ("I", "me", "my")** — tracks *self-focus* and, counter-intuitively, *low status* and *distress*. The person in pain, the depressed, the subordinate, the truth-teller under scrutiny all use more "I". The high-status, the confident, and (often) the liar use *less*.
- **First-person plural ("we")** — solidarity, shared identity — but also distancing ("we need to talk"), and the royal/editorial "we" of evasion.
- **Second person ("you")** — engagement, but also blame and generalisation ("you just feel helpless" = distancing from one's own "I feel").
- **Articles and concrete nouns ("the", "a")** — high use marks concrete, categorical, object-focused thinking (correlates with analytic, often male, often higher-status framing).
- **Cognitive-mechanism words ("because", "think", "realise", "cause", "know")** — signal active reappraisal and sense-making; a rise in these over time (e.g. in journaling) tracks recovery and insight.
- **Negations, exclusives ("but", "without", "exclude") and tentativeness ("maybe", "perhaps")** — mark distinction-drawing, honesty complexity, or hedging.
- **Verb tense** — a shift into past tense marks distancing from an event; present tense marks immersion.
- **Emotion-word density and valence** — direct affect readout.

The status heuristic in one line: in any dyad, **the higher-status person uses fewer "I"-words and more "we/you"-words; the lower-status person uses more "I".** (Pennebaker's email-analysis finding — it flips with the direction of the relationship.)

**Vivid exemplars.** A useful *generative* tool more than a spot-the-character one. Give the depressive an "I"-saturated idiolect and a poverty of "we". Give the grandiose narcissist a suspicious *scarcity* of "I" and a flood of categorical "the"-noun pronouncements. Write the liar's account with fewer self-references, fewer exclusives, and more distancing — then let a sharp POV character half-notice the wrongness without being able to name it (the DePaulo problem, below). Render a couple falling in love by the rise of "we". Show a recovering griever by the slow return of causal "because"-words to their speech.

**What it gives you for character depth.** A *microscopic, unfakeable* channel that operates beneath everything the character consciously performs — the perfect vehicle for `self-and-perception.md`'s gap between self-image and truth: a man performs confidence in content words while his function words leak the subordinate's "I". It gives the narrator a way to show state and status *without stating them*, and it gives the interior a measurable spillover into the surface — the exact bridge this file is about.

**Combines-with.** Directly instruments **status/tenor** (register, above; the Circumplex agency axis, `trait-models.md`) and **state** (distress → "I"; `state-engine.md`). "I"-saturation is the linguistic face of high Panksepp PANIC-GRIEF / low mood (`generative-model.md`). Function-word leakage is a **thin-slicing** cue (below) — but a weak, statistical one, which is why observers rarely consciously read it. Cross-links to the **recorded lie** in `event-semantics` (a lie is thought≠speech; function words are where the effort of lying faintly shows).

**Cautions.** (1) *Effect sizes are small and statistical* — these are population regularities, not per-utterance diagnostics; a single "I" proves nothing. Use them to *shape a voice's texture over many lines*, not as a decoder ring. (2) *Do not make it visible machinery* — the power is that it works below notice; a character who announces "you keep saying 'I'" breaks the spell. (3) The status/"I" finding is robust but context-bound (dyadic, English); don't over-globalise. (4) LIWC-style analysis is a research instrument, not a lie detector — its deception signal is real but far too weak to convict a character in-world (see DePaulo).

**Sources.** Pennebaker, *The Secret Life of Pronouns* (2011); Tausczik & Pennebaker, "The psychological meaning of words: LIWC and computerized text analysis" (*Journal of Language and Social Psychology*, 2010); Chung & Pennebaker, "The psychological functions of function words" (2007); Kacewicz et al., "Pronoun use reflects standings in social hierarchies" (*JLSP*, 2014).

## Pragmatics — the cooperative principle, implicature, and face

**Name and lineage.** **H.P. Grice**, "Logic and Conversation" (1975) — the cooperative principle and conversational maxims; **Erving Goffman**, "On Face-Work" (1955) — face as the public self-image; **Penelope Brown & Stephen Levinson**, *Politeness: Some Universals in Language Usage* (1978/1987) — politeness theory built on face. The study of *meaning beyond the literal* — what an utterance *does* and *implies*, not just what it says.

**Core idea.** People mean more than they say, and *how* they say it manages the relationship. Grice: conversation runs on an assumed cooperative principle and four maxims; speakers routinely *flout* the maxims on purpose, and listeners compute the intended meaning (the **implicature**) from the gap between what was said and what cooperation required. Brown & Levinson: every person has **face** — a public self-image — and many acts *threaten* it, so speakers wrap requests, criticisms, and refusals in politeness strategies calibrated to power, distance, and the size of the imposition. A character's *pragmatic style* — how they flout, hedge, threaten face, or refuse to — is one of the most individuating things about their voice.

**Structure in full.**

*Grice's maxims (the cooperative default):*
- **Quantity** — say as much as needed, no more. (Flout: pointed under-informing, or a flood of detail that itself signals evasion.)
- **Quality** — say what you believe true and can support. (Flout: irony, sarcasm, metaphor, hyperbole — "Oh, *brilliant*." — all say the false to mean the true.)
- **Relation (relevance)** — be relevant. (Flout: the abrupt change of subject that *means* "we are not discussing that.")
- **Manner** — be clear, brief, orderly, unambiguous. (Flout: deliberate obscurity, the euphemism, the pointed circumlocution — meaning conveyed by *not* saying it plainly.)

**Implicature** is what the flout communicates. Most wit, subtext, threat, and tenderness in dialogue lives here — the character says X and means Y, and the reader (and the addressee) does the arithmetic. This *is* subtext, mechanised.

*Goffman / Brown & Levinson — face:*
- **Positive face** — the want to be liked, approved of, included, seen as a good member.
- **Negative face** — the want to be unimpeded, autonomous, not imposed upon.
- **Face-threatening act (FTA)** — any move that endangers either (a request threatens negative face; a criticism threatens positive face).

*The politeness strategies, by increasing mitigation:*
1. **Bald on-record** — no mitigation ("Give me the gun."). Used with great power difference, emergency, or intimacy where face is not at stake.
2. **Positive politeness** — appeal to solidarity, flatter the hearer's positive face ("You're so good at this — help me out?").
3. **Negative politeness** — defer, apologise, hedge, minimise the imposition ("Sorry to bother you, I don't suppose you could…?").
4. **Off-record** — hint, imply, leave deniable ("It's cold in here." = shut the window). Maximum face-safety, maximum deniability.
5. **Don't do the FTA at all** — the unsaid request.

The choice among these is a function of **Power** (hearer's power over speaker), **social Distance**, and the **Rank of the imposition** — Brown & Levinson's weighting: the bigger the P, D, or R, the more mitigation. So *a character's default politeness level, read against the actual P/D/R, tells you exactly how they see the relationship.*

**Vivid exemplars.** Jane Austen is the master of off-record implicature — an entire courtship conducted in flouted-quantity understatement, and Mr Darcy's first proposal is a catastrophe of *insufficient* negative politeness (he does the FTA bald-on-record with a superior who has power he's misjudged). Hemingway's "Hills Like White Elephants" is nearly *all* implicature — the abortion is never named; the whole story is flouted relevance. A hard man who says "Nice shop. Be a shame if something happened to it." is off-record threat — deniable menace, the entire *Sopranos* register. Sherlock Holmes flouts *quantity* constantly (dazzling over-informative deductions) as dominance display. The bureaucrat who buries a refusal in negative-politeness padding ("we regret that we are unable, at this time…") vs the blunt boss who says "No." — two whole characters in the choice of strategy.

**What it gives you for character depth.** Subtext with a *mechanism* — you can build a line that means the opposite of its words and know *why* the reader will get it. Politeness strategy is a direct readout of a character's *read of a relationship's power and distance* — so a mismatch (bald-on-record to someone who outranks them; elaborate negative politeness to a subordinate) is instant characterisation: arrogance, fear, sarcasm, or a misjudged room. It is the pragmatic engine behind the whole *others-see* view: characters infer status and intent from *how* things are said.

**Combines-with.** Face and P/D/R map straight onto the **Interpersonal Circumplex** (`trait-models.md`) — agency = who does FTAs bald-on-record, communion = which face they protect. Politeness level is the fine grain of **register tenor** (above). Flouting requires and reveals **theory of mind** (`emotion-and-decision`: the speaker models the hearer's inference) — a character who *can't* read implicature (takes everything literally) is a specific, often autistic-coded or alien voice. HEXACO **Honesty-Humility** predicts *sincere* pragmatics (low use of flattery-as-positive-politeness); low-H manipulators are virtuoso face-workers. Speech-act force is treated as the *recorded event* in `event-semantics` (illocution not perlocution) — this section is the *stylistic* face of that machinery.

**Cautions.** (1) *Implicature depends on shared ground* — an off-record hint the addressee (or reader) can't compute is just noise; the flout only works if the cooperative baseline is visible. (2) Brown & Levinson's *universality* claim is contested cross-culturally (positive-face vs negative-face weighting varies by culture — a high-negative-face Anglo strategy reads as cold in a high-positive-face culture); use it as a *lens*, and let culture set the defaults (`worldbuilding-frameworks`). (3) Over-cleverness — a cast where *everyone* speaks in dazzling implicature flattens into one authorial wit; most people flout clumsily or not at all. (4) Sarcasm-on-the-page is fragile without tonal cues; lean on situation and the addressee's reaction to carry it.

**Sources.** Grice, "Logic and Conversation" (in *Syntax and Semantics 3*, 1975); Brown & Levinson, *Politeness: Some Universals in Language Usage* (1987); Goffman, "On Face-Work" (*Psychiatry*, 1955); Levinson, *Pragmatics* (1983); Leech, *Principles of Pragmatics* (1983).

## Conversational style and turn-taking — how a person occupies the floor

**Name and lineage.** Two adjacent traditions. **Conversation Analysis (CA)** — **Harvey Sacks, Emanuel Schegloff & Gail Jefferson**, "A simplest systematics for the organization of turn-taking for conversation" (*Language*, 1974): the turn-taking machine, adjacency pairs, preference organisation, repair. And **interactional sociolinguistics** — **Deborah Tannen**, *Conversational Style: Analyzing Talk Among Friends* (1984) and *That's Not What I Meant!* (1986): the **high-involvement ↔ high-considerateness** contrast, built on Goffman's interaction order (*Forms of Talk*, 1981) and Gumperz's contextualisation cues.

**Core idea.** Register is *which variety* a person speaks; pragmatics is *what a line does*; conversational style is *how a person takes part in the exchange itself* — how they seize, hold, and yield the floor, whether they overlap or wait, how fast they talk, how long they let a silence run, how they signal "your turn now". These are low-awareness habits, and the load-bearing dramatic fact is that **the friction is cross-style, not in either style alone**: two people each behaving normally by their own norms each read the other as rude. This is a distinct voice channel — two characters with the same idiolect and register can be opposite in how they occupy talk.

**Structure in full.**

*The turn-taking machinery (Sacks/Schegloff/Jefferson):* talk is built turn-by-turn at **transition-relevance places** (points where a turn could complete); the norms push toward *one speaker at a time* with gaps and overlaps minimised — so a habitual overlapper or a long-pauser is heard as *marked*. The current speaker may select the next, or the next self-selects. **Adjacency pairs** (question→answer, greeting→greeting, invitation→accept/decline) set up a slot the second part is expected to fill; a **dispreferred** second (a refusal, a disagreement) comes delayed, hedged, prefaced, and accounted-for — its very *shape* leaks the reluctance before any word does ("well… I mean, it's not that I wouldn't…"). **Repair** has an order too: self-correction is preferred, and being other-corrected is a small face-loss.

*Tannen's two styles (a spectrum, not a binary):*
- **High-involvement** — fast pace, cooperative overlap and latching (no gap between turns), expressive prosody, faster questions, personal topics, "talking along" *as* enthusiasm and rapport. Overlap here is warmth, not interruption.
- **High-considerateness** — slower pace, longer tolerated pauses, one-at-a-time, waits for a clear turn-end, hears overlap as an offence, gives more conversational space.
Neither is objectively more polite. Deployed among sharers, each works. Across styles it misfires: the involvement speaker fills the considerateness speaker's pause and is heard as steamrolling; the considerateness speaker never claims the floor and is heard as having nothing to say.

*The individuating floor-dials:* pace and tempo; pause tolerance; overlap-as-rapport vs overlap-as-attack; who initiates vs who follows; topic control (who steers, who merely supplies); backchannel rate ("mm-hm", nods); questions-as-interest vs questions-as-interrogation; the reflexive apology vs the reflexive challenge; who gets to leave a silence unfilled.

**Vivid exemplars.** The *Gilmore Girls'* latched, overlapping, machine-gun involvement style *is* the bond — an outsider cannot get a word in. Sorkin's walk-and-talk is high-involvement floor-holding as competence display. Tannen's own finding: New Yorkers' cooperative overlap read as rude by slower-paced Californians at a dinner — identical goodwill, opposite reception. The interrogator who weaponises silence, refusing to fill the pause so the suspect fills it for him. The committee where one member's considerateness-pauses are permanently colonised by another's involvement-overlaps — dominance enacted through nothing but turn-taking. A dispreferred "well, look, it's complicated, the thing is…" that declines an invitation without ever saying no.

**What it gives you for character depth.** A voice channel *beneath* word choice: the difference between two characters can live entirely in the *shape* of the exchange — overlap, latch, pause, interruption — which the reader registers instantly without any line being individually distinctive. Style *mismatch* is characterisation-through-conflict that needs no villain (both parties are behaving well by their own lights). And turn-taking is where **power** is enacted moment to moment — who interrupts whom and gets away with it is a live status readout, not a stated fact.

**Combines-with.** Turn-taking power is the **Interpersonal Circumplex** agency axis in motion (`trait-models.md`) and the "power markers" of the **verbal signature** (below) made systematic. Pace and overlap deform under **state** — arousal quickens tempo and raises interruption rate (`state-engine.md`, `emotion-and-decision`). Backchannels and turn-signals are the **regulators** of the nonverbal channel (below). Dispreferred-turn shaping is **negative politeness / face** (pragmatics, above) enacted in real-time sequence. Style is **culturally** set (`worldbuilding-frameworks`) — a world can give its peoples distinct floor-norms and stage the clash as intergroup friction.

**Cautions.** (1) *Style is not personality* — a high-involvement speaker is not "dominant"; among sharers it is warmth. Read it as style-in-context, and let the *mismatch* carry the meaning. (2) On the page, rendering overlap and latching is a *typographic* problem (em-dashes, dropped attributions, run-together lines, the double-dash cut-off) — deploy it sparingly or the exchange turns to mush. (3) Tannen's regional/ethnic style labels are population tendencies with enormous individual variation — do not let a style become a stereotype-stamp on a person. (4) CA describes what talk *does*; it is not a rulebook to enforce — use adjacency-pair and repair structure to *shape* a beat, not to mechanise dialogue.

**Sources.** Sacks, Schegloff & Jefferson, "A simplest systematics for the organization of turn-taking for conversation" (*Language*, 1974); Tannen, *Conversational Style: Analyzing Talk Among Friends* (1984) and *That's Not What I Meant!* (1986); Schegloff, *Sequence Organization in Interaction* (2007); Goffman, *Forms of Talk* (1981).

## The verbal signature — engineering a voice recognisable in one line

**Name and lineage.** A craft synthesis rather than a single theory — the practical distillation of the above (register, function words, pragmatics) into the writer's working question. Its lineage is dramatic and editorial: the theatrical tradition of the "character line," Dickens's tag-phrases, the screenwriting doctrine that you should be able to cover the character names in a script and still know who's talking, and narratology's treatment of *distinct voice* (`narrative-craft`: heteroglossia, skaz, Bakhtin's many-voiced text).

**Core idea.** A recognisable character has a **verbal signature**: a small, consistent bundle of features that makes a line of theirs identifiable *without attribution*. The signature is not a costume of quirks — it is the *audible shape of the interior*. Each feature should trace to something (a trait, a fear, a class, a wound), so that the voice is a *readout* of the person, not decoration. Depth test: strip the dialogue tags from a scene; if the reader still knows who speaks, the voices are real.

**Structure in full — the components of a signature (author a distinctive *few*, not all):**
- **Lexicon** — pet words, characteristic diction level (Latinate vs Anglo-Saxon — `narrative-craft`), a private vocabulary, a trade's jargon, an era's slang, oaths and their flavour ("gods" vs "Christ" vs "hell").
- **Syntax and length** — clipped fragments vs rolling periodic sentences; parataxis (and-and-and) vs hypotaxis (subordinated, qualified); the person who never finishes a sentence vs the one who never starts a short one.
- **Rhythm and cadence** — the music of the line (the meter of a formal speaker; the staccato of a frightened one).
- **Discourse markers and fillers** — "look", "so", "I mean", "y'know", "right?", "as it were" — the throat-clearings that fingerprint a speaker.
- **Verbal tics and catchphrases** — a repeated construction (Jules's cadence; a repeated question; a signature curse) — most memorable, most easily overdone.
- **Pragmatic habit** — the person who always flouts quantity (over-explains), always hedges (negative politeness reflex), always goes bald-on-record, always answers a question with a question.
- **Power markers** (`narration.md`; conversational style, above) — who interrupts, who yields the floor, who commands vs requests, who uses names as leverage, who apologises reflexively.
- **What they *won't* say** — the topic always deflected, the word never used, the register never reached (the man with no intimate register; the woman who cannot say "sorry"). Absence is signature.
- **Paralanguage rendered on the page** — pace, pauses, the trailing-off, the flat affect, volume — carried by punctuation and beat, not adverbs.

The *engine*: pick two or three features that spring from the character's core (a fearful controller over-hedges and over-qualifies; a low-H charmer front-loads positive politeness and names; a grief-frozen man speaks in short declaratives and never uses "we"), keep them consistent, and let *state* deform them (the controlled voice fractures under enough load — a caused tail-sample, `trait-models.md` Whole Trait Theory).

**Vivid exemplars.** Rorschach's tenseless, article-dropped journal-speak ("Dog carcass in alley this morning") is a whole worldview in a grammar. Yoda's inverted syntax; Jules Winnfield's scripture-and-cadence; Hagrid's warmth-in-dialect; Deadwood's Al Swearengen fusing Shakespearean periodicity with obscenity; Holden Caulfield's "phony" and "and all" and "if you want to know the truth"; Bertie Wooster's breezy hypotaxis against Jeeves's precise negative politeness; Gollum's split idiolect. Each is one line and you know them. The counter-example is instructive: a cast where everyone speaks in the author's own wit has *no* signatures — one voice in many mouths.

**What it gives you for character depth.** This is the literal answer to the file's governing question — *recognisable in a single line*. It converts all the interior machinery into the one thing a reader continuously experiences: the sound of a specific mind. And because each feature is anchored to a cause, the signature is also a *diagnostic surface* — when it deforms, the reader feels the state-change before it's named (the unshakeable voice that finally cracks).

**Combines-with.** Draws its raw material from **register/idiolect**, **function words**, and **pragmatics** (all above); its craft rendering (diction, cadence, free indirect discourse, distinct voices) is `narrative-craft`'s department. Its deformation-under-load is the **state engine** and Whole Trait Theory (`trait-models.md`); its anchoring-to-cause is the `generative-model.md` rule that the surface is a *sample*, never a field. Power markers feed and read the **Circumplex** and **relationships** (`relationships.md`).

**Cautions.** (1) *Tic ≠ character* — a catchphrase with no interior behind it is a mascot, not a person; and over-repetition turns a signature into a punchline (the one-note comic-relief voice). (2) *Consistency is the whole game* — a signature that drifts un-caused reads as the author forgetting the voice; a signature that *never* deforms reads as a robot. (3) Don't give *everyone* a loud signature — real ensembles have a couple of vivid voices and several plainer ones; wall-to-wall quirk is exhausting. (4) The signature must survive translation of *content* — if you can only tell the character by their catchphrase, you've built a tag, not a voice; the syntax and pragmatics should carry them even when the catchphrase is absent.

**Sources.** McKee, *Dialogue* (2016); Egri, *The Art of Dramatic Writing* (1946); Bakhtin, *The Dialogic Imagination* (1981, on heteroglossia); Leech & Short, *Style in Fiction* (2007); and the pragmatic/sociolinguistic sources above.

## Nonverbal channels — kinesics, proxemics, and the body's grammar

**Name and lineage.** **Ray Birdwhistell** coined *kinesics* (body motion, 1952); **Edward T. Hall** coined *proxemics* (use of space, *The Hidden Dimension*, 1966) and pioneered the study of time-as-communication, coining *monochronic*/*polychronic* (*The Silent Language*, 1959) — though the umbrella term *chronemics* was later coined by **Fernando Poyatos** (1972) and mapped out by **Thomas Bruneau** (late 1970s), not by Hall; **Paul Ekman & Wallace Friesen** produced the canonical taxonomy of nonverbal behaviour (1969). **Albert Mehrabian**'s much-abused 7-38-55 figures (*Silent Messages*, 1971) belong here as both a datum and a caution.

**Core idea.** The body speaks in parallel with the voice, largely below awareness, across several distinct channels. Much of it is *illustrative and regulatory* (it organises talk), some is *emotional leakage* (it betrays state the speaker would hide), and some is *emblematic* (culturally coded signs). For a writer, the body is where interior state becomes *witnessable* — the raw material a POV narrator can actually render and a thin-slicing observer actually reads.

**Structure in full.**

*Ekman & Friesen's five categories of nonverbal behaviour:*
- **Emblems** — gestures with a direct verbal translation, culturally specific: thumbs-up, the OK ring, the middle finger, a beckoning finger. Consciously deployed; meaning is dictionary-fixed within a culture (and dangerously *different* across them).
- **Illustrators** — movements that accompany and depict speech: batons (beat the rhythm/emphasis), pictographs (draw the shape in air), deictics (point), kinetographs (mime an action), ideographs (trace the path of a thought). Largely unconscious, tied to the flow of talk; their *absence* (a still speaker) is itself a signal.
- **Affect displays** — mostly facial, the expressions of emotion; can be spontaneous or managed, and are subject to **display rules** (culturally learned norms for masking, intensifying, or neutralising feeling in company).
- **Regulators** — the traffic-signals of conversation: nods, eye contact shifts, posture leans, "mm-hm"s that manage turn-taking, invite, or cut off. How two bodies negotiate the floor.
- **Adaptors** — self- or object-directed manipulations that manage tension, mostly unconscious and *not* intended to communicate — but they leak: self-adaptors (nail-biting, neck-rubbing, hair-touching), object-adaptors (pen-clicking, ring-twisting), alter-adaptors (arm-crossing, protective distancing). The classic "tell" lives here.

*Hall's proxemic zones (Western/North-American norms — culture-relative):*
- **Intimate** (~0–18 in / 0–45 cm) — lovers, children, close comfort or physical threat.
- **Personal** (~1.5–4 ft) — friends and family, comfortable conversation.
- **Social** (~4–12 ft) — acquaintances and formal/business interaction.
- **Public** (~12 ft+) — speaker-to-audience, public figures.
A violated zone is instant drama: the stranger who stands too close, the lover who steps back into social distance, the interrogator who invades intimate space to dominate.

*The other channels:*
- **Haptics** — touch: the handshake's grip, the withheld embrace, the hand-on-shoulder that claims status, the slap.
- **Oculesics** — gaze: eye contact (its duration a dominance/intimacy signal), the look away, the stare, pupil dilation.
- **Vocalics / paralanguage** — the *how* of the voice: pitch, volume, tempo, timbre, pauses, filled hesitations ("um"), sighs, the tremor. (Register's audible carrier.)
- **Posture and orientation** — open vs closed, leaning-in vs leaning-away, the turned shoulder, who faces whom.
- **Chronemics** — time as message: who is kept waiting, who arrives early, monochronic (one-thing-at-a-time, punctual) vs polychronic (many-things, relationship-over-clock) cultures (Hall's distinction).
- **Appearance and artifacts** — dress, grooming, adornment as deliberate and leaked signals (bleeds into thin-slicing / behavioural residue, below).

*The Mehrabian figures* — in judgements of **feeling and attitude**, when word, tone, and face are *incongruent*, listeners weighted them roughly **7% words / 38% tone / 55% face**. This is the *only* claim the study supports.

**Vivid exemplars.** The interrogator crowding intimate space; the aristocrat who never raises her voice because volume is for people who lack power; a man whose words say "I'm fine" while his object-adaptor (turning a wedding ring) leaks the opposite; the handshake contest between two alphas; the averted gaze of shame vs the held gaze of challenge; the character who is *always* early (chronemic anxiety) vs the one who makes everyone wait (chronemic dominance); Mr Darcy's stiff proxemic reserve read as pride. On the page, the body is often the *tell* that contradicts the line — the POV sees the hand, mis- or half-reads it, and the reader gets the irony.

**What it gives you for character depth.** The renderable surface — a POV-bounded narrator (`narration.md`) can only report what is *witnessed*, and the body is what's witnessed. Nonverbal leakage is the primary way interior state (Layer 7) becomes *legible to others* without the character stating it — and the primary way `self-and-perception.md`'s *others-see* view gets its (often wrong) data. Proxemics and haptics are the physical grammar of the **relationship** layer; adaptors are the tell that opposes the spoken lie.

**Combines-with.** Affect displays are the facial output of the Panksepp primaries (`generative-model.md`) and the input to the emotion-recognition / theory-of-mind read (`emotion-and-decision`). Proxemic/postural dominance and gaze are the **Circumplex** agency-communion axes made physical (`trait-models.md`). Adaptor frequency scales with **state** arousal and allostatic load (`state-engine.md`, `emotion-and-decision`: arousal narrows and agitates). Display rules are set by **culture and position** (`worldbuilding-frameworks`, `character-anatomy.md` Layer 9). The FACS/leakage detail is the next section.

**Cautions.** (1) **The Mehrabian myth** — "93% of communication is nonverbal" is a gross misuse; the figures apply *only* to incongruent feeling-messages in a narrow experiment, not to communication in general. Cite it correctly or not at all. (2) *Nonverbal meaning is culture-relative and context-relative* — an emblem safe in one culture is obscene in another; crossed arms may mean defensiveness *or* cold *or* comfort. There is no reliable body-language "dictionary"; treat single cues as ambiguous and let clusters + context carry meaning. (3) *Adaptors are tension, not lies* — a fidget signals arousal, whose *source* (fear, excitement, guilt, caffeine) the observer must infer and often gets wrong (see DePaulo, next). (4) On the page, drowning dialogue in choreographed gesture ("she said, tucking her hair, shifting her weight, glancing away") is beat-clutter — select the *one* load-bearing tell.

**Sources.** Ekman & Friesen, "The repertoire of nonverbal behavior: categories, origins, usage, and coding" (*Semiotica*, 1969); Hall, *The Hidden Dimension* (1966) and *The Silent Language* (1959); Birdwhistell, *Kinesics and Context* (1970); Mehrabian, *Silent Messages* (1971); Knapp, Hall & Horgan, *Nonverbal Communication in Human Interaction* (2013).

## The face, microexpressions, leakage — and the limits of tell-reading

**Name and lineage.** **Paul Ekman** and colleagues: the cross-cultural studies of facial emotion (1970s), the **Facial Action Coding System** (FACS, Ekman & Friesen, 1978), and the leakage/microexpression hypothesis; **Bella DePaulo** et al.'s decisive meta-analysis of deception cues (2003), which is the honest counterweight to the whole "reading people" mythos.

**Core idea.** The face is the richest emotional channel, and *some* of its signal escapes voluntary control — hence "leakage": brief, involuntary displays (microexpressions) that flash the felt emotion through a mask. This is real. What is *not* real is the popular belief that liars have reliable tells and trained observers can catch them: the actual cues to deception are, in DePaulo's words, *"faint"* — weak, few, and easily swamped by individual differences and stress. Both facts matter for character: emotion genuinely leaks, but *reading* it accurately is hard and error-prone, which is dramatic gold.

**Structure in full.**

*The face:*
- **FACS** — a taxonomy of ~40+ **Action Units** (independent muscle movements: AU12 = lip-corner puller/smile, AU4 = brow lowerer/frown, AU1+2 = raised inner+outer brow, etc.). A purely descriptive coding of *what the face does*, from which emotion inferences are (contestably) drawn. Its value to a writer is precision: the *Duchenne* (genuine) smile engages the orbital muscles around the eyes (AU6) as well as the mouth; the social/false smile is mouth-only. "The smile didn't reach her eyes" is FACS in prose.
- **Microexpressions** — very brief (a fraction of a second) full-face emotional displays that leak a suppressed feeling before the mask reasserts. The basis for the *leakage hypothesis*: the true feeling flashes, then the display rule covers it.
- **Display rules** — the learned management (mask / neutralise / amplify / substitute) that sits *over* the involuntary display; the gap between felt and shown is precisely where drama lives.

*The deception reality (DePaulo et al., 2003 — meta-analysis of 120 independent samples; ~1,300 estimates of 158 cues in all, ~50 of them examined in ≥5 studies):*
- Of the ~50 cues examined in ≥5 studies, only **14 (~28%)** showed *any* significant link to lying, and their average effect was tiny (**d ≈ .25**). "The looks and sounds of deceit are faint."
- The *slightly* more reliable cues are **not** the folk ones (gaze-aversion, fidgeting are near-useless): liars tend to be **less forthcoming**, tell **less compelling / less detailed** stories, include **fewer ordinary imperfections and unusual details**, make a **more negative impression**, and appear **more tense** — cognitive-load and impression-management effects, not a Pinocchio's-nose.
- Human lie-detection accuracy hovers near **chance (~54%)**, and "experts" (police, judges) are barely better while being *more confident* — the dangerous combination.

**Vivid exemplars.** The widow whose grief is a flawless mask save one microexpression of relief that the POV *almost* catches and cannot interpret. Tom Ripley's controlled face — Highsmith lets us inside, so we see the mask *chosen*. The interrogator who is *certain* he can read the suspect and is confidently, catastrophically wrong (the DePaulo finding as plot). The card player's Duchenne-vs-social smile. A parent who amplifies delight at a bad gift (display-rule substitution) while the child, too young for display rules, leaks pure disappointment. The *Lie to Me* / Cal Lightman archetype — dramatically thrilling, scientifically overstated, and best used *with* an awareness that its confidence is itself a flaw.

**What it gives you for character depth.** Two gifts. First, **leakage** — a disciplined way for suppressed interior state to escape onto the witnessable surface (the felt emotion the character is *managing* flashes through), which is the engine of dramatic irony when a POV sees the flash and misreads it. Second, and rarer, the **epistemic humility** of the deception literature: it authorises *unreliable social reading* as the norm, not the exception. Characters who confidently read others and are wrong; observers who catch the *tension* but attribute it to the wrong cause; the truth that a nervous innocent and a calm liar look identical — all of this is *validated*, and it powers the `self-and-perception.md` "others-see is often wrong" design.

**Combines-with.** Leakage is the involuntary tail of **affect displays** (previous section) and the facial output of the Panksepp basis (`generative-model.md`). Display rules are **culture/position**-set (`worldbuilding-frameworks`). The deception-cue weakness is the crucial constraint on **thin-slicing** (next) and on the *others-see* vault (`self-and-perception.md`) — it says observers are noisy sensors. Cognitive-load cues (less detail, more tension) tie to the **state engine** and Yerkes-Dodson/Easterbrook load effects (`emotion-and-decision`). The *recorded* lie (thought≠speech) is `event-semantics`' business; this section is why *catching* it is unreliable in-world.

**Cautions.** (1) **The tell is a myth** — do not write a character who reliably detects lies from gaze/fidgets; that's folklore, and DePaulo is the citation to prove it. If a character *does* read a liar, make it *content*-based (the story doesn't hang together) or luck, not a magic microexpression. (2) *Microexpressions are real but rare and hard to catch in real time* — use leakage sparingly and let it be *ambiguous* (relief? or fear? or nausea?). (3) The **classical view of basic emotions with universal facial signatures is contested** (Barrett's constructed-emotion critique, `emotion-and-decision`) — "the fear face" is a strong default, not a law; context disambiguates a face far more than the face alone. (4) Beware the *competence-halo* on the human lie-detector — real ones don't exist; the confident reader is a *flaw* to exploit, not a superpower to grant.

**Sources.** Ekman, *Telling Lies* (1985/2009) and *Emotions Revealed* (2003); Ekman & Friesen, *Facial Action Coding System* (1978); DePaulo et al., "Cues to deception" (*Psychological Bulletin*, 2003); Bond & DePaulo, "Accuracy of deception judgments" (*Personality and Social Psychology Review*, 2006); Vrij, *Detecting Lies and Deceit* (2008); and Barrett et al. (2019) for the critique.

## Cognitive styles and thinking dispositions — the shape of a mind

**Name and lineage.** A cluster of individual-difference constructs describing *how* (not *how well*) a person thinks: **Herman Witkin**'s field dependence–independence (1940s–70s); **Michael Kirton**'s Adaption–Innovation theory (KAI, 1976); **John Cacioppo & Richard Petty**'s Need for Cognition (1982); **Arie Kruglanski**'s Need for Cognitive Closure (1993); **Seymour Epstein**'s Cognitive-Experiential Self-Theory (rational vs experiential systems, 1994); **E. Tory Higgins**'s Regulatory Focus (promotion/prevention, 1997); **Richard Nisbett**'s holistic–analytic cultural cognition (*The Geography of Thought*, 2003); **Robert Sternberg**'s mental self-government thinking styles (1988); **Jerome Kagan**'s reflectivity–impulsivity (1965).

**Core idea.** People have characteristic, cross-situational *styles* of perceiving, deciding, and reasoning — separable from ability and from the Big Five, and often more visible in behaviour than either. A style is value-neutral (each pole is adaptive somewhere) and it shows up constantly: in how a character sizes up a room, tolerates ambiguity, seeks or avoids novelty, decides fast or slow, sees the object or the field. Cognitive style is the *texture of thought* the reader infers from how a character approaches problems — a huge, under-used characterisation channel.

**Structure in full — the load-bearing styles:**
- **Field independence ↔ field dependence** (Witkin). Field-*independent* people separate an object from its context, restructure a field, rely on internal referents — analytical, impersonal, good at "seeing through" clutter. Field-*dependent* people perceive holistically, are more attuned to the social field and to context, better at reading people and rooms. Neither is smarter; it is a perceptual-cognitive orientation. (The detective who abstracts the clue from the scene vs the one who feels the emotional temperature of the household.)
- **Adaptor ↔ Innovator** (Kirton, KAI). *Adaptors* prefer to do things *better* within the existing paradigm — precise, reliable, improve the system, work inside the box. *Innovators* prefer to do things *differently* — challenge the paradigm, generate proliferation of ideas, break the box (and irritate the adaptors). A *level*-independent *style*: a brilliant adaptor and a brilliant innovator differ in kind, not competence, and clash on every committee.
- **Need for Cognition — high ↔ low** (Cacioppo & Petty). High-NFC people *enjoy* effortful thinking, seek out puzzles, elaborate. Low-NFC people find cognition aversive and lean on heuristics, cues, and others' judgements. (The character who turns everything over vs the one who wants the bottom line.)
- **Need for Cognitive Closure — high ↔ low** (Kruglanski). High-NFCC people crave a firm answer and hate ambiguity — they **seize** on early information and **freeze** on it (premature closure, black-and-white certainty, discomfort with open questions). Low-NFCC people tolerate and even enjoy ambiguity, keep options open, resist premature commitment. This is one of the most *characterful* dials — the dogmatist vs the perpetual doubter.
- **Rational ↔ Experiential** (Epstein, CEST — a dual-process *style*). Some people habitually run the slow, analytic, rule-based system; others trust the fast, affective, intuitive, story-and-image system. A trait-level version of System 2 vs System 1 (`emotion-and-decision`).
- **Promotion ↔ Prevention focus** (Higgins). Promotion-focused people pursue *gains, ideals, aspirations* with *eager* strategies (chase upside, tolerate errors of commission). Prevention-focused people pursue *security, duties, oughts* with *vigilant* strategies (avoid downside, tolerate errors of omission). Same goal, opposite emotional and strategic colouring — hope-and-elation vs relief-and-vigilance.
- **Analytic ↔ Holistic cognition** (Nisbett). Analytic thinkers (the tendency Nisbett associates with Western traditions) focus on focal objects, categorise by rules, apply formal logic, attribute cause to the object's dispositions. Holistic thinkers (associated with East-Asian traditions) attend to the whole field and relationships, expect change and contradiction (dialectical reasoning), attribute cause to context. A *cultural*-cognitive style — hugely useful for making a world's peoples *think* differently, not just dress differently.
- **Reflectivity ↔ impulsivity** (Kagan). The speed/accuracy trade-off under uncertainty: reflective people scan and deliberate before answering; impulsive people answer fast and err more. (Distinct from Conscientiousness — a *tempo* of decision.)
- **Sternberg's mental self-government** — thinking *styles* modelled on forms of government: legislative (create/invent), executive (implement/follow), judicial (evaluate/judge); plus scope (internal/introverted vs external/people-oriented) and leaning (liberal/novelty vs conservative/routine). A palette for *preferred cognitive role*.

**Vivid exemplars.** Sherlock Holmes: field-independent, high-NFC, low-NFCC-in-reasoning-but-impatient, judicial-legislative. Hamlet is high-NFC + *low* closure + reflective to the point of paralysis — the tragedy is a cognitive style. Captain Ahab is *maximum* closure (seized and frozen on the whale) and pure promotion-focus obsession. A prevention-focused character is the cautious quartermaster who survives because he plans for loss; the promotion-focused one is the founder who bets the company. Kirton's split writes the whole "maverick vs the institution" story — the innovator hero against the adaptor bureaucracy (and, honestly, the adaptors are often *right*). A holistic-thinking character in a Nisbett-divided world reads a conflict as a web of relationships where the analytic character sees two culpable individuals.

**What it gives you for character depth.** A channel that is neither trait nor value nor emotion — the *how* of thought, which shows up in every problem-solving beat and is largely invisible to the character themselves (people don't know their own style). It differentiates two equally smart, equally conscientious characters by the *shape* of their reasoning; it makes intellectual conflict character-based (adaptor vs innovator, high vs low closure) rather than just plot-based; and it lets a `worldbuilding-frameworks` culture install a *cognitive* signature, so its people think in a recognisable idiom.

**Combines-with.** Styles cut across HEXACO but aren't identical to it: NFC and Openness correlate (~.3) but aren't the same; NFCC relates to low Openness + high Conscientiousness-Orderliness but adds the *seize-and-freeze* dynamic. Rational-vs-experiential is the *trait* face of the dual-process machinery in `emotion-and-decision` (System 1/2, the somatic marker). Promotion/prevention maps onto the drives schema's **coping_engagement** (approach/avoid) and onto Gray's BAS/BIS (`trait-models.md`). Closure and reflectivity govern *how a character enters a decision* before the values weighting resolves it (`decision-engine.md`). Holistic/analytic sets **attribution** style (next section — holistic thinkers make fewer fundamental attribution errors).

**Cautions.** (1) **Learning styles (VAK — visual/auditory/kinaesthetic) are a debunked myth** — there is no evidence that matching instruction to a "learning style" helps; do not confuse the discredited *learning-styles* industry with the legitimate *cognitive-styles* constructs here. (2) Many style constructs *overlap* each other and the Big Five — don't author six redundant dials; pick the one that names the texture you need (the repo's sweet-spot caution, `character-model.md`). (3) Several have modest psychometrics (field dep/indep blurs into spatial *ability*; Sternberg's styles are lightly validated) — use them as evocative lenses, not measurements. (4) Nisbett's East/West contrast is a population tendency with large individual variation and real critique — use it to *diversify* how peoples think, never to stereotype an individual. (5) Style is value-neutral by design — resist making the innovator the hero and the adaptor the drone; the reliable adaptor is often the one who's *right*.

**Sources.** Witkin & Goodenough, *Cognitive Styles: Essence and Origins* (1981); Kirton, *Adaption-Innovation* (2003); Cacioppo & Petty, "The need for cognition" (*JPSP*, 1982); Kruglanski & Webster, "Motivated closing of the mind: seizing and freezing" (*Psychological Review*, 1996); Epstein, "Integration of the cognitive and psychodynamic unconscious" (*American Psychologist*, 1994); Higgins, "Beyond pleasure and pain" (*American Psychologist*, 1997); Nisbett, *The Geography of Thought* (2003); Sternberg, *Thinking Styles* (1997); Pashler et al., "Learning styles: concepts and evidence" (*Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 2009, the debunk).

## Locus of control and explanatory style — how a person narrates cause

**Name and lineage.** **Julian Rotter**'s Locus of Control (1954/1966; the I-E Scale) — the repo's committed Layer-3 orientation (`drives-schema.md`, `values-and-stakes.md`); **Hanna Levenson**'s three-way refinement (Internality / Powerful Others / Chance, 1973); **Bernard Weiner**'s attribution theory (1972/1985; the causal dimensions of achievement); **Martin Seligman** and colleagues' reformulated learned-helplessness / **explanatory style** (1978 onward; *Learned Optimism*, 1991). All concern the *stories people tell about why things happen* — to them and around them.

**Core idea.** When something happens — success, failure, loss, luck — a person automatically explains it, and the *shape* of that explanation is a stable individual difference that governs their emotional and behavioural response far more than the event itself. Do they see themselves as the author of outcomes or the object of fate? Is a setback *their* fault, permanent, and everywhere — or external, temporary, and local? These attributional habits are a mind's characteristic *causal grammar*, and they are the single best predictor of how a character metabolises what befalls them. The repo already makes **locus of control** a first-class lever precisely because it governs the response to anything that happens *to* a character (`values-and-stakes.md`'s son-dies example: internal-locus → self-blame + "I must act"; external → fate + acceptance).

**Structure in full.**

*Rotter — locus of control (one axis):*
- **Internal** — outcomes flow from one's own actions, effort, ability, choices. Correlates with agency, initiative, information-seeking, resilience *and* self-blame; the person who *acts on and fixes* the world.
- **External** — outcomes flow from luck, fate, powerful others, circumstance. Correlates with acceptance, fatalism, lower stress-of-responsibility *and* lower initiative; the person who *endures and adapts to* the world.

*Levenson's three sub-scales (splitting "external"):*
- **Internality** — belief one controls one's own life.
- **Powerful Others** — belief that specific powerful people/institutions control outcomes (a *social* externality — cynicism, deference, or paranoia).
- **Chance** — belief that fate/luck/randomness controls outcomes (a *cosmic* externality — fatalism, superstition, "what will be will be").
(The distinction matters: the character who thinks *the bosses* run everything is different from the one who thinks *the gods* do.)

*Weiner's three causal dimensions (a fuller attributional space):*
- **Locus** — internal (ability, effort) vs external (task difficulty, luck).
- **Stability** — stable/permanent (ability, task difficulty) vs unstable/temporary (effort, luck). Governs *expectancy* — attributing failure to a stable cause kills hope; to an unstable one preserves it.
- **Controllability** — controllable (effort) vs uncontrollable (ability, luck). Governs *emotion and blame* — failure attributed to controllable causes → guilt/shame and others' anger; to uncontrollable → pity.
Each combination yields a distinct emotional signature (failure = low ability [internal-stable-uncontrollable] → shame and hopelessness; failure = low effort [internal-unstable-controllable] → guilt but hope).

*Seligman's explanatory style (the "3 P's", applied to good vs bad events):*
- **Personal** (internal ↔ external) — "it's me" vs "it's circumstances".
- **Permanent** (stable ↔ temporary) — "always" vs "this time".
- **Pervasive** (global ↔ specific) — "everything" vs "just this".
**Pessimistic style:** bad events are personal, permanent, pervasive ("I failed because I'm worthless, I always will, and my whole life is ruined") — the depressive/helpless engine. **Optimistic style:** bad events are external, temporary, specific; *good* events get the reverse (personal, permanent, pervasive). The asymmetry *is* the optimism.

Related attributional biases that flavour the whole system: the **self-serving bias** (my successes are internal, my failures external), the **fundamental attribution error** (others' behaviour is dispositional, mine is situational — see next section), and **just-world belief** (people get what they deserve — which turns misfortune into evidence of the victim's fault).

**Vivid exemplars.** Two soldiers survive the same ambush: the internal-locus one interrogates every choice he made and resolves to train harder (guilt + agency); the external-locus one calls it the will of God and sleeps (acceptance). Job is a theological argument about explanatory style. A pessimistic-style protagonist reads one rejection as proof of total, permanent unlovability (personal-permanent-pervasive) — the depressive spiral rendered as attribution. Boxer in *Animal Farm* — "I will work harder" — is internal-locus + controllable-effort attribution weaponised by a system that exploits it. A just-world-believing character who *cannot* accept that the innocent suffered, and so blames the victim, is a specific and chilling moral failure. The self-serving-bias character who takes credit for every win and blames the team for every loss writes his own arc.

**What it gives you for character depth.** The mechanism for *reaction to circumstance* — which, in a repo that directs characters by circumstance rather than force (`dramatic-structure`), is the whole game. The same event (a death, a loss, a betrayal) produces opposite behaviour depending on the causal story the character tells, and that story is stable, authorable, and largely invisible to them. It converts "how does she take the news?" from an ad-hoc choice into a *derivation* from her attributional style. And it powers arcs: an arc from pessimistic to optimistic explanatory style (or the reverse) is a measurable, dramatizable interior change.

**Combines-with.** This is the repo's **Layer-3 orientation** (`drives-schema.md`: `locus_of_control`, `agency`) expanded — Levenson and Weiner add the sub-structure, Seligman adds the good/bad asymmetry. Attribution style shapes **emotion** via appraisal (`emotion-and-decision`: locus/controllability *are* appraisal dimensions in OCC, Weiner, Scherer) — so explanatory style is where the appraisal engine's *dispositional bias* lives. Pessimistic style co-occurs with high Panksepp PANIC-GRIEF / FEAR and low mood (`generative-model.md`). Holistic cognition (previous section) reduces the fundamental attribution error (holistic thinkers attribute more to context). Internal-locus + high-agency amplifies HEXACO Conscientiousness and drives goal-pursuit (`drives-schema.md`).

**Cautions.** (1) *Neither pole is "healthy"* — extreme internal locus produces crushing self-blame and control-obsession (the parent who thinks he could have prevented the unpreventable); extreme external produces passivity. The *depressive realism* wrinkle: mild pessimists are sometimes more *accurate*. Don't moralise the axis. (2) *Locus is domain-specific* — a person can be internal about work and external about love; a single global score flattens a real texture (Levenson's split helps). (3) Explanatory style is culturally shaped — fatalism is adaptive and rational in genuinely uncontrollable circumstances (`worldbuilding-frameworks`: a peasant under famine is not "irrational" to be external). (4) Don't let the character *narrate their own attribution style* aloud — it should show in *how they explain events in passing*, not in a self-diagnosis.

**Sources.** Rotter, "Generalized expectancies for internal versus external control of reinforcement" (*Psychological Monographs*, 1966); Levenson, "Multidimensional locus of control" (1973); Weiner, *An Attributional Theory of Motivation and Emotion* (1986); Abramson, Seligman & Teasdale, "Learned helplessness in humans" (*Journal of Abnormal Psychology*, 1978); Seligman, *Learned Optimism* (1991).

## Cognitive biases as character quirks — the idiolect of thought

**Name and lineage.** The **heuristics-and-biases** program: **Amos Tversky & Daniel Kahneman** (1974 onward; *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, 2011), with the wider literature (Nisbett & Ross, Gilovich, Ariely, Thaler). The decision-mechanics of these biases belong to `emotion-and-decision`; *this* section treats them from the other end — as **stable, recognisable habits of a particular mind**, the cognitive equivalent of a verbal tic.

**Core idea.** A bias is not just an error the average person makes — its *strength* varies between people and is fairly stable within a person, so a characteristic bias-profile is a real individuating feature. One character *always* over-weights the vivid anecdote (availability); another *always* reads ambiguous behaviour as hostile (hostile attribution); another *always* trusts the confident (authority/halo). Written as a *consistent* filter on how a character perceives and reasons, a bias becomes a **quirk of mind** as recognisable as a quirk of speech — and, crucially, an *engine of error* that drives plot without the character being stupid (smart people have strong, stable biases).

**Structure in full — a curated catalog, framed as character filters:**
- **Confirmation bias** — seeks and weights evidence that fits an existing belief, discounts the rest. *Character:* the detective locked onto the wrong suspect, the jealous spouse assembling a case, the ideologue. Drives the tragic mistaken-certainty plot.
- **Fundamental attribution error** — reads others' behaviour as *character*, one's own as *circumstance*. *Character:* the quick-to-judge moralist ("he's lazy"; "I was tired"). The engine of misjudged others and of the `self-and-perception.md` gap.
- **Self-serving bias** — credit for success, external blame for failure (see previous section). *Character:* the man who is never at fault.
- **Anchoring** — over-relies on the first number/frame encountered. *Character:* the negotiator anchored by an opening offer, the mind that can't escape a first impression.
- **Availability heuristic** — judges likelihood by how easily examples come to mind (recent, vivid, emotional). *Character:* the parent terrified of rare dangers after one news story; the veteran who sees the last war in every threat.
- **Representativeness** — judges by resemblance to a prototype, ignoring base rates. *Character:* the profiler who trusts the stereotype; the stock-picker fooled by a good story.
- **Sunk-cost fallacy** — throws good resources after bad because of prior investment. *Character:* the general who can't retreat, the lover who won't leave "after all these years", the gambler chasing losses.
- **Optimism bias / planning fallacy** — underestimates risk and time-to-complete for one's own plans. *Character:* the overreaching schemer, the "it'll be fine" leader marching into disaster.
- **Negativity bias** — bad looms larger than good; one betrayal outweighs ten kindnesses. *Character:* the grudge-holder, the wound that won't heal (the repo's relationship-update negativity bias, `relationships.md`).
- **Dunning-Kruger** — low competence + low insight into that incompetence = unwarranted confidence; and its inverse, the expert's *under*-confidence (impostor feeling). *Character:* the confident fool, the self-doubting master (the self<others gap, `self-and-perception.md`).
- **Hindsight bias** — "I knew it all along"; the past looks inevitable after the fact. *Character:* the second-guesser, the "I always said so" relative.
- **Hostile attribution bias** — reads ambiguous acts as intentionally hostile. *Character:* the aggressive, the paranoid, the trauma-shaped hypervigilant (a specific, clinically real filter).
- **Halo effect** — one salient good trait (beauty, confidence, one competence) colours the whole judgement. *Character:* the room that trusts the tall handsome liar; the mark charmed by the con.
- **In-group / out-group bias** — over-credits the tribe, over-suspects the other. *Character:* the loyalist, the bigot, the factional partisan (ties to Moral-Foundations loyalty, `values-and-stakes.md`).
- **Illusion of control / superstition** — over-estimates one's causal reach (the ritual before the battle). *Character:* the compulsive planner, the gambler with a system.

The organising insight: a character usually carries *one or two dominant biases that spring from their core* (the wounded man's negativity bias; the low-H charmer who exploits *others'* halo effect; the grief-locked mother's availability-driven terror), plus the universal human baseline. A *consistent* dominant bias is a diagnostic surface — it tells the reader what the character *can't see*, which is where dramatic irony and self-inflicted plot both come from.

**Vivid exemplars.** Othello is a confirmation-bias engine — Iago supplies the frame, and Othello assembles only confirming evidence to a murderous certainty. Captain Ahab: sunk cost + illusion of control fused into monomania. Mr Darcy and Elizabeth: *mutual* fundamental attribution error (the novel is literally titled for two biases) corrected over the arc — bias-as-arc. Gatsby: optimism bias + the planning fallacy of remaking the past. Miss Havisham: negativity bias crystallised into a stopped clock. The paranoid noir protagonist's hostile-attribution filter. Michael Scott (*The Office*) as ambulatory Dunning-Kruger.

**What it gives you for character depth.** A way to make a *smart* character *wrong* believably and consistently — error that flows from a stable filter rather than authorial fiat or stupidity, which is the only kind of character-mistake that reads as earned. It converts "why didn't she see it?" into a *derivation* from her dominant bias. It gives dramatic irony a mechanism (the reader sees what the character's bias blinds them to). And a bias *corrected* is a clean, legible arc (P&P), while a bias *indulged to ruin* is a clean tragedy (Othello).

**Combines-with.** The decision-mechanics of every bias here live in `emotion-and-decision` (this is the *characterological* face of that toolbox — cross-reference, don't duplicate the mechanics). Bias *strength* is a stable individual difference that composes with HEXACO (low Openness ↔ stronger confirmation/closure; high Emotionality ↔ stronger negativity/threat biases) and with **cognitive style** (high Need-for-Closure amplifies confirmation and anchoring; holistic cognition dampens the fundamental attribution error). Negativity bias is wired into the **relationship-update** rule (`relationships.md`). Attribution biases are the previous section's territory seen as *error*. A bias is a *belief-formation* distortion, so it operates on the **vault** (`knowledge-model.md`) — it shapes what a character comes to believe from what they witness.

**Cautions.** (1) *A bias is not stupidity* — the whole point is that intelligent, expert people carry strong stable biases; writing bias as dumbness misses it (and insults the character). (2) *Don't stack too many* — a character riddled with every bias is incoherent; pick the one or two that spring from their wound/trait and let those be consistent. (3) *Biases are contested and context-dependent* — many "biases" are ecologically rational shortcuts that fail only in the lab (Gigerenzer's critique, `emotion-and-decision`); a heuristic that misfires in one setting is adaptive in another, so let the *situation* make the bias a virtue or a flaw. (4) *Don't let the character name their bias* — it should show in how they reason, not in "ah, my confirmation bias again." (5) The replication crisis touched parts of this literature (ego-depletion, some priming/anchoring effects) — use the robust, phenomenologically obvious ones and hold the shaky ones lightly.

**Sources.** Tversky & Kahneman, "Judgment under uncertainty: heuristics and biases" (*Science*, 1974); Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011); Nisbett & Ross, *Human Inference* (1980); Gilovich, Griffin & Kahneman (eds.), *Heuristics and Biases* (2002); Gigerenzer, *Gut Feelings* (2007, the counter-case).

## Thin-slicing and the lens model — how the interior surfaces and gets read

**Name and lineage.** **Nalini Ambady & Robert Rosenthal**'s "thin slices" research (1992, 1993), popularised by **Malcolm Gladwell** (*Blink*, 2005); **Egon Brunswik**'s **lens model** (1956) as the formal framework; **Samuel Gosling**'s work on **behavioural residue** and personality-from-environments (*Snoop*, 2008; "A room with a cue", 2002); the **zero-acquaintance** accuracy literature (Funder, Kenny, Vazire — the SOKA/RAM models); **Frank Bernieri** and colleagues on interpersonal perception. This closes the loop: how the interior (everything else in the toolbox) becomes surface, and how observers turn a sliver of surface back into a whole-person judgement.

**Core idea.** Observers form startlingly confident and *often accurate* personality judgements from tiny exposures — a two-second clip, a photo, a bedroom, a handshake. They do it by reading **cues**: observable features (a firm grip, a messy desk, a fast gait, a smile, a word choice) that are *statistically* linked to the underlying trait. The **lens model** formalises the whole transaction: the trait "broadcasts" through a lens of imperfect cues, and the observer reconstructs it through the same lens. Accuracy depends on two independent things: whether the cues *actually* track the trait (**cue validity**) and whether the observer *uses* the valid ones (**cue utilisation**). Because both can fail, thin-slice judgements are a mix of uncanny accuracy and confident error — which is exactly the material of the `self-and-perception.md` *others-see* view.

**Structure in full.**

*The lens model (Brunswik) — the transaction in four terms:*
- The **distal variable** — the true, hidden trait (e.g. Extraversion).
- The **proximal cues** — observable signals it emits (loud speech, expansive gesture, quick approach, a stylish stimulating room).
- **Cue validity** — how strongly a cue *actually* correlates with the trait (loud speech genuinely tracks Extraversion; a firm handshake weakly tracks it).
- **Cue utilisation** — how strongly the *observer* leans on a cue when judging (people over-use some cues, under-use others).
- **Achievement (accuracy)** = the match between the trait and the judgement, which is high only when observers utilise *valid* cues (**cue sensitivity**). Misjudgement comes from using *invalid* cues (stereotypes, halo) or ignoring valid ones.

*Which traits thin-slice well (validity is not uniform):*
- **Extraversion** is read fastest and most accurately from thin slices (it is loud, expansive, visible).
- **Conscientiousness** reads well from *behavioural residue* (an organised space, punctuality, grooming) more than from a two-second clip.
- **Openness** reads best from *environments and artifacts* (a distinctive, varied, book-and-art-filled room — Gosling's strongest environmental finding).
- **Agreeableness** and especially **Neuroticism** are read *poorly and slowly* — they are internal, maskable, and cue-poor; strangers are near-chance on Neuroticism. (This asymmetry is gold: your character's warmth or anxiety is the *last* thing a stranger reliably reads.)

*Gosling's two cue-sources:*
- **Identity claims** — deliberate statements of who one is (posters, slogans, displayed trophies, chosen clothes, a curated shelf) — can be *aspirational* and misleading.
- **Behavioural residue** — the *unintended* physical traces of actual behaviour (the alphabetised CDs, the unwashed dishes, the worn path, the charcoal sketches on the floor) — harder to fake, often more valid. The forensic reading of a room *is* character revealed by residue.

*Zero-acquaintance findings:* strangers agree with each other and with self-reports well above chance after minimal exposure, especially on Extraversion and Conscientiousness; consensus rises with the target's *observability* and the cue's *validity* (Funder's Realistic Accuracy Model: accurate judgement requires cues that are *relevant, available, detected, and correctly utilised* — four points where it can break).

**Vivid exemplars.** Sherlock Holmes reading a stranger's whole biography off residue (mud on the boots, a tan line, a callus) — the lens model as superpower, valid cues utilised perfectly (and, honestly, over-sold). The detective who walks a victim's apartment and reads the life off the shelves and the state of the bed. A first-meeting where the POV thin-slices a charming stranger as trustworthy (high cue utilisation on invalid halo cues) and is wrong — the con works *because* thin-slicing is fast and fallible. The interview panel that hires the confident extravert over the anxious competent (Extraversion's high visibility crowding out the low-validity read of Conscientiousness). A character who *curates* identity claims (the aspirational bookshelf, the borrowed accent) to project a self their residue would contradict — the whole `self-and-perception.md` self-image-vs-truth gap staged in a room.

**What it gives you for character depth.** This is the *mechanism of the surface* — the formal account of how everything interior in this toolbox becomes witnessable cue, and how another mind reconstructs (or misreconstructs) a person from it. It tells you *which* surface features to foreground (the high-validity, high-visibility cues — Extraversion and residue read fast; warmth and anxiety hide), and it validates *systematic misjudgement*: the con, the underestimated introvert, the mistrusted innocent, the halo-charmed room. It gives the *others-see* vault its sensor model (thin-sliced, cue-limited, confidently wrong) and gives the writer a principled way to plant a mislead — show the reader the invalid cue the POV over-uses.

**Combines-with.** The lens model is the formal engine under `self-and-perception.md`'s *others-see* view and under the repo's "strangers thin-slice observable actions in seconds" claim (`character-model.md`, `character-anatomy.md` Layer 8). The *cues* are everything in this file (register, function words, nonverbal channels, face) plus appearance and residue; the *distal traits* are HEXACO (`trait-models.md`). Cue *utilisation* errors are the **biases** of the previous section (halo, representativeness, fundamental attribution error) operating on person-perception. The weak deception-cue finding (DePaulo, above) is a *special case* of low cue validity. Thin-slice reads feed the **relationship** first-impression prior (`relationships.md`) and are corrected (or not — negativity bias, primacy) by subsequent evidence. The objective-correlative and "show don't tell" (`narrative-craft`) are the *authorial* use of the same lens — the writer plants the valid cue and lets the reader thin-slice.

**Cautions.** (1) *Thin-slicing is fast **and** fallible* — Gladwell's *Blink* oversells its accuracy; the same speed that nails Extraversion misses Neuroticism and is captured by stereotype. Write both the hits and the confident misses. (2) *Cue validity is trait-specific and often low* — don't grant a character reliable reads of *internal* traits (warmth, anxiety, honesty) from a glance; those are genuinely cue-poor (the honest limit). (3) *Identity claims lie* — a curated surface (the room, the outfit, the accent) can be aspirational; residue is the harder-to-fake tell, and the *gap* between claim and residue is character. (4) The forensic-genius reader (Holmes, the profiler) is a genre convention that over-states real cue validity — thrilling, but flag it as heightened, and remember DePaulo. (5) Thin-slice accuracy is culturally and contextually bound; cues valid in one world are noise in another (`worldbuilding-frameworks`).

**Sources.** Ambady & Rosenthal, "Thin slices of expressive behavior as predictors of interpersonal consequences" (*Psychological Bulletin*, 1992); Brunswik, *Perception and the Representative Design of Psychological Experiments* (1956); Gosling et al., "A room with a cue: personality judgments based on offices and bedrooms" (*JPSP*, 2002); Gosling, *Snoop* (2008); Funder, "On the accuracy of personality judgment: a realistic approach" (*Psychological Review*, 1995); Kenny, *Interpersonal Perception* (1994); Gladwell, *Blink* (2005, the popularisation — and its own caution).

## How to reach into this file

Start from what the moment on the page needs, not from a favourite framework:

- **Making a character recognisable in one line** → the **verbal signature** (a distinctive few of: register range, function-word texture, pragmatic habit, syntax/cadence, power markers, and what they *won't* say) — anchored so each feature is the audible shape of an interior cause, never a free-floating tic.
- **Placing a person socially in a sentence / shifting the temperature of a scene** → **register and idiolect** (Joos's five clocks, Halliday's field/tenor/mode); watch the *shift* — it reads state and relationship.
- **Writing subtext, threat, wit, or tenderness that means the opposite of its words** → **pragmatics** (Grice's flouted maxims → implicature; Brown & Levinson's face and politeness strategies read against power/distance).
- **Staging conflict or power in the exchange itself — who interrupts, overlaps, waits, or is talked over** → **conversational style and turn-taking** (Sacks/Schegloff/Jefferson's turn machine and dispreferred-turn shaping; Tannen's high-involvement vs high-considerateness, where the *mismatch* is the drama).
- **Leaking status, distress, or a lie beneath the performed surface** → **function words** (the "I"-load of the subordinate and the distressed; the liar's thinner self-reference) — statistical texture over many lines, never a decoder ring.
- **Rendering interior state as witnessable behaviour** → the **nonverbal channels** (Ekman/Friesen's five types, Hall's proxemic zones, gaze/touch/vocalics) and **facial leakage** (Duchenne vs social smile, the microexpression through the mask) — select the *one* load-bearing tell; cite Mehrabian correctly or not at all.
- **Having a character read another (and be right, or wrong)** → **thin-slicing and the lens model** (valid vs used cues; Extraversion reads fast, warmth and anxiety hide; identity claims lie, residue tells) — and the **deception limits** (DePaulo: tells are faint, confident readers are a *flaw*).
- **Giving a mind a recognisable *style of thought*** → **cognitive styles** (field dep/indep, adaptor/innovator, need for cognition, need for closure's seize-and-freeze, promotion/prevention, holistic/analytic) — the *how* of thinking, largely invisible to the character, that differentiates two equally smart people.
- **Deriving how a character metabolises what happens *to* them** → **locus of control and explanatory style** (the repo's Layer-3 orientation, expanded — Rotter/Levenson, Weiner's three dimensions, Seligman's 3 P's): the same event, opposite response, from a stable causal grammar.
- **Making a smart character wrong, believably and consistently** → **cognitive biases as quirks** (one or two dominant biases that spring from the wound/trait — confirmation, negativity, sunk-cost, hostile-attribution, halo) — the filter that shows the reader what the character can't see.

**The through-line:** the surface is a *sample of the interior* (`generative-model.md`), and every framework here is a channel through which the stored person (HEXACO disposition, Schwartz/Moral-Foundations worth, the Panksepp affective basis, the drives-schema orientation) becomes *witnessable cue* — voice carrying identity and stance, body leaking state, the mind's style and biases shaping perception — which another mind then thin-slices back into a (frequently wrong) judgement. Author the interior deep; author the surface as its faithful, deformable readout; foreground the high-validity cues; let observers read imperfectly; and let the reader supply the trait-name after the line. That misread gap — between what a character *is*, what they *show*, and what others *take them for* — is not noise to be cleaned up. It is, per `self-and-perception.md`, the character.
