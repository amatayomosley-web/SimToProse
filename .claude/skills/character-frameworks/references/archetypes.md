# Archetypes — Depth-Psychological and Narrative

The oldest and most widely shared way of naming a kind of person is the **archetype**: the Hero, the Shadow, the Wise Old Man, the Trickster, the Mother, the Outlaw — figures that recur across myth, dream, fairy tale, scripture, and every screenplay ever pitched with "she's the mentor, he's the shapeshifter." This file gathers that vast well and sorts it, because "archetype" is used for two genuinely different things that are constantly conflated. One is a **depth-psychological** claim — that beneath individual personality lie universal, inherited patterns of the psyche (Jung's Self, Shadow, Anima/Animus, Persona, and the twelve-archetype systems built on them); these describe a character's *interior*. The other is a **narrative-functional** claim — that a story deploys a small cast of *roles* defined not by who a character is but by what they *do to the plot* (Campbell's mythic roles, Vogler's eight, Propp's seven dramatis personae, Dramatica's eight archetypal characters); these describe a character's *job in the machine*. The distinction is load-bearing for this project, so the file keeps it in front of you throughout: a psychological archetype is a **bias-pack over the repo's dimensional store** (HEXACO leanings + value weights + Panksepp baselines + drives), exactly like `type-typologies.md`'s types; a narrative archetype is a **function**, closer to the dramatic-structure and relationship layers than to the interior — and crucially, *one character can wear a different narrative mask in every scene while their psychology stays fixed*. Read the whole file as a designer's palette, framework-neutral and example-rich; be candid, as the sections are, about where the universality claims outrun the evidence. The repo's defaults — HEXACO, Schwartz + Moral Foundations, the seven Panksepp primaries, the drives schema — remain the dimensional backbone; archetypes are the fastest way to *conjure* a whole person or cast a whole story, which you then dissolve into that backbone where behavior is actually produced.

## Where the repo sits — archetypes as bias-packs (interior) and functions (role)

Before the well, the house position, so the cross-links land. The project **stores and resolves behavior dimensionally**, never by archetype label:

- **Disposition** is HEXACO — six factors (Honesty-Humility, Emotionality, eXtraversion, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, Openness), each a `(mean, variability)` distribution, resolved by sampling (`docs/trait-theory.md`).
- **Worth** is Schwartz's ten values + Moral Foundations + SDT needs — a shared menu the person weights (`docs/values-and-stakes.md`).
- **Affect** is the seven Panksepp primaries — SEEKING, FEAR, RAGE, CARE, PANIC/GRIEF, LUST, PLAY — the action-dispositional basis emotions and virtues are *generated* from, not stored (`docs/generative-model.md`).
- **Motivation** is the drives schema — per-person goals, fears/wounds, and orientation (locus, coping), all opposable (`docs/drives-schema.md`).

Two kinds of archetype map onto that spine two different ways, and keeping them apart prevents most misuse:

1. **Psychological archetypes** (Jung's four; the Pearson/Mark twelve; the cross-cultural interior figures) are **regions in the dimensional space plus a characteristic dynamic** — the same shape as a `character-model.md` model preset. "The Caregiver" is not a field; it is a compact name for {high CARE baseline, benevolence-weighted worth, a fear of being unwanted/unloved, a `please`/`perform` defense}. So the workflow is the same as for the typologies: **archetype in, dimensions out** — conjure a coherent soul fast, then translate the gestalt into HEXACO leanings, value weights, primary-emotion baselines, and drives, because that is what the decision engine runs on, and because a stored archetype-label re-imports every validity problem catalogued below.

2. **Narrative-role archetypes** (Campbell/Vogler's mythic roles; Propp's dramatis personae; Dramatica's eight) are **not interior at all** — they are **functions a character performs in the plot and in relation to the protagonist**. "Mentor," "Threshold Guardian," "Herald," "Antagonist" describe a *job*: what this character *does to the hero's journey*. These map onto the **dramatic-structure** skill (the plot machine) and the **relationships** model (edges to the protagonist), *not* onto HEXACO/Panksepp. The decisive fact: **role is scene-local and reassignable** — the ally becomes the traitor (shapeshifter), the mentor becomes the threshold guardian, one person plays herald in act one and shadow in act three — while their *psychology* (the dimensional store) never changes. A character *is* a psychology; they *play* a role. Never store a narrative role as a personality attribute; store it as a relationship/plot function that the scene assigns.

The rest of the file walks the well. Psychological systems first (interior bias-packs), then narrative systems (plot functions), then the cross-cultural layer that feeds both — with the through-line that **an archetype is a lens and a scaffold, never the operand the engine resolves.**

## Jung's foundational archetypes — the Self, the Shadow, the Anima/Animus, the Persona

**Name and lineage.** Carl Gustav Jung, developed across the *Collected Works* (esp. vol. 9i, *The Archetypes and the Collective Unconscious*, 1934–54; *Aion*, 1951; *Two Essays on Analytical Psychology*, 1943). Jung split from Freud partly over this: beneath the personal unconscious (repressed biography) he posited a **collective unconscious** — an inherited, species-wide substratum populated by **archetypes**, innate organizing forms (not fixed images) that structure how humans perceive, feel, and imagine. The idea has deep antecedents (Platonic Forms, Kant's categories, Augustine's *archetypus*) and was carried forward by Marie-Louise von Franz, Erich Neumann (*The Origins and History of Consciousness*, *The Great Mother*), James Hillman (archetypal psychology), and the whole post-Jungian tradition. The concept is scientifically unfalsifiable and empirically contested; it is nonetheless one of the most generative vocabularies for character and story ever produced.

**Core idea.** An archetype is an inherited *predisposition to form* certain images and roles — a psychic organ, like the eye is an organ predisposed to form vision. We do not inherit the image of the Mother; we inherit the readiness to organize experience around a Mother-form, which our culture then fills with content. The archetypes manifest as recurring figures in myth and dream, and — Jung's key clinical claim — as **structural parts of every individual psyche** that must be reckoned with for wholeness (*individuation*). The four below are the load-bearing ones for characterization because they describe the architecture of a *single* self: a public mask, a disowned dark, a contrasexual inner other, and a total-self toward which the whole system strives.

**Structure in full.** The four core structural archetypes (plus the wider catalog):

- **The Persona** — the *social mask*, the face turned to the world; the compromise between the individual and society about "how one should appear." Named for the mask of Greek theater. Necessary and healthy in measure (we all need a professional self, a public self); pathological when one *identifies* with it and loses the person beneath — the man who is only his role, hollow when the office is taken away. The Persona is what others are meant to see; its danger is inflation (believing the mask) and its collapse is a character crisis. **Maps to the repo's `view: self` / others-see split** (`docs/self-and-perception.md`): the Persona is precisely the curated others-see surface, and the gap between it and truth-view is dramatic gold.

- **The Shadow** — everything the ego has disowned, repressed, or refused to recognize in itself: not merely "the evil within" but *all* the unlived, unadmitted material — cowardice, cruelty, greed, but also unexpressed talent, unclaimed power, forbidden desire. "Everyone carries a shadow, and the less it is embodied in the individual's conscious life, the blacker and denser it is." The Shadow is met first through **projection** — the traits we cannot own we see, hatefully, in others; the person who enrages us disproportionately is carrying our Shadow. Integration (owning the Shadow, "making the darkness conscious") is the first and hardest task of individuation. Dramatically: the antagonist is often the hero's Shadow externalized (the thing he could become, refuses to see, must face). **Maps to the disowned material of `docs/self-and-perception.md`'s three views and to the whole `shadow-and-clinical.md` register** — the Shadow is the psychodynamic parent of that file's self-deception engine.

- **The Anima / Animus** — the *contrasexual* inner figure: the **Anima**, the feminine image in a man's unconscious; the **Animus**, the masculine image in a woman's. (Jung's heteronormative, historically bound framing — read it in modern terms as the *disowned-gendered / inner-other* function, the complement one is not consciously identified with, regardless of the person's gender or orientation.) The Anima/Animus governs relationship and mood, is projected onto love-objects (falling in love = projecting one's Anima/Animus onto a real person), and is the **bridge to the deeper unconscious** — the psychopomp, the inner guide toward the Self. In story it appears as the mysterious beloved, the muse, the fatal woman, the guiding inner voice — the *soul-image* that both misleads (projection) and leads (individuation). Neumann and later Jungians developed its developmental stages (Eve→Helen→Mary→Sophia for the Anima, etc.).

- **The Self** — the *archetype of wholeness and the regulating center* of the total psyche, conscious and unconscious together; not the ego (which is only the center of consciousness) but the superordinate totality toward which the psyche strives. Symbolized by mandalas, the circle, the *quaternity*, the Divine Child, the wise king, Christ and Buddha figures — images of unity-of-opposites. **Individuation** is the lifelong process of the ego relating to the Self, integrating Shadow and Anima/Animus, and becoming the whole one *is* rather than the fragment one *shows*. Narratively, the Self is what a completed character-arc *arrives at* — the integrated person on the far side of the journey.

*The wider catalog (the "archetypes" as recurring figures, beyond the four structural ones):* Jung and the post-Jungians named many more archetypal figures and motifs — **the Wise Old Man** (Senex; the mana-personality, the guru/mentor/magician who dispenses meaning), **the Great Mother** (nurturing *and* devouring — Neumann's dual mother), **the Divine Child** (the *puer*, vulnerable futurity, the redeemer-in-embryo), **the Maiden/Kore**, **the Trickster** (the collective shadow, undifferentiated consciousness — see the cross-cultural section), **the Hero**, **the Father**, **the Wounded Healer**, and archetypal *events* (birth, death, initiation, the sacred marriage) and *motifs* (the deluge, the world-tree, the descent to the underworld). These are the raw stock the twelve-archetype systems (next) and the mythic-role systems (below) systematize.

**Vivid exemplars.** *Persona:* Don Draper's flawless ad-man surface over a stolen identity; any character who is "on" until the door closes. *Shadow:* the Jungian reading of the Hulk (Banner's disowned rage); Gollum as Sméagol's Shadow; the Emperor as Luke's potential-Shadow ("you'll join me"); Jekyll's Hyde as pure split Shadow; Fight Club's Tyler Durden as the narrator's Shadow made flesh. *Anima:* Dante's Beatrice (the guiding soul-image); the femme fatale as *negative* Anima projection (Vertigo's Madeleine); Ayesha ("She") in Haggard. *Animus:* the idealized/demonized masculine an aggrieved character projects; the inner "voice of judgment." *Self:* the mandala-completion of a finished arc — Frodo integrated (and wounded) at the Grey Havens; the Fisher-King healed; any "wholeness on the far side of the descent." *Wise Old Man:* Gandalf, Dumbledore, Yoda, Merlin, Obi-Wan. *Great Mother:* Demeter (nurturing) and Kali/Baba Yaga (devouring) as the two faces.

**What it gives you for character depth.** The single richest vocabulary for a character's *interior architecture and its conflict* — not what they're like on a scale, but the *drama between the parts of one self*: the mask vs. the face beneath (Persona/Shadow), the disowned dark met in projection (Shadow), the soul-image that both seduces and guides (Anima/Animus), and the whole-self a completed arc reaches (individuation as the master arc-shape). It hands you an **arc engine** (individuation = integrate Shadow + Anima/Animus toward the Self) and a **villain theory** (the antagonist as the hero's externalized Shadow) that are still the most-used story bones in the industry. It is unrivalled for rendering *self-conflict without a second character in the room* — the interior cast of Persona, Shadow, and Anima is a drama of parts.

**Combines-with.** The Persona is the repo's **others-see / curated self-view** (`docs/self-and-perception.md`), the Persona/face gap = the `view: self` split. The Shadow is the disowned truth-view material and the psychodynamic root of `shadow-and-clinical.md`'s self-deception and defense mechanisms (projection *is* the immature defense). The Shadow-as-antagonist connects to the **dramatic-structure** skill's antagonist-mirrors-protagonist principle. Anima/Animus feeds `docs/relationships.md` (projection onto love-objects biases how a bond is read and built) and connects to attachment (the idealized inner other). The Self / individuation is the master **arc shape** (`docs/arc-engine.md`) — the completed character-arc *is* an individuation. Beebe's eight-function model (`type-typologies.md`) is a direct elaboration: it assigns Jung's archetypal roles (Hero, Anima, Trickster, Demon) to the eight cognitive functions.

**Cautions.** (1) **Empirically unfalsifiable.** The collective unconscious and inherited archetypes are not testable and are rejected by mainstream psychology as literal claims; their standing is as a *hermeneutic and generative* framework, not a science. Do not narrate them as fact (a modern character who *believes* in archetypes is characterization; the repo does not endorse the metaphysics). (2) **The Anima/Animus framing is dated and gendered** — Jung's essentialism about "the feminine" and "the masculine" is a historical artifact; translate to the disowned-inner-other function and drop the biology. (3) **Shadow-inflation cliché:** "the villain is literally the hero's shadow-self" is now so overused (evil twin, dark mirror) it can read as thin; use the *mechanism* (projection, disowned material) more than the literal doppelgänger. (4) **Everything-is-an-archetype over-reading:** because the vocabulary is elastic, any character can be labeled any archetype post hoc — treat it as an interpretive/generative language, never a measurement. (5) The Persona is *healthy and necessary* — do not write every mask as pathological hypocrisy.

**Sources.** Jung, *The Archetypes and the Collective Unconscious* (CW 9i, 1959) and *Aion* (CW 9ii, 1951); Jung, *Two Essays on Analytical Psychology* (CW 7); Erich Neumann, *The Origins and History of Consciousness* (1949) and *The Great Mother* (1955); Marie-Louise von Franz, *Shadow and Evil in Fairy Tales* (1974); Anthony Stevens, *Archetype Revisited* (2003) — a modern, evolutionarily-informed defense.

## The Twelve Archetypes (Pearson, and Pearson & Mark) — the interior twelve

**Name and lineage.** A twelve-archetype system of *core human motivations*, distinct from the Jungian four (which are structural parts of one psyche) — these twelve are *whole character-types*, each a complete package of desire, strategy, gift, and fear. Developed by **Carol S. Pearson** across *The Hero Within* (1986, six archetypes) and *Awakening the Heroes Within* (1991, expanded to twelve), as a **developmental** model — archetypes we move *through* as guides on the journey of life. Popularized for branding (and thereby for character work everywhere) by **Margaret Mark & Carol S. Pearson**, *The Hero and the Outlaw* (2001), which arranged the twelve on a wheel by four underlying motivations. The lineage runs Jung → Campbell → Pearson → Mark; the branding application made it ubiquitous, which is both its reach and its cliché risk. (Note: the exact twelve names vary slightly by source — Everyman/Regular Guy/Orphan; Outlaw/Rebel; Magician/Wizard — the set is stable, the labels drift.)

**Core idea.** Each archetype is organized around a **core desire**, pursues it via a **strategy**, offers a **gift/talent**, and flees a **basic fear**; that quartet is nearly identical in shape to the repo's drives schema (a terminal goal's `serves`, a defense, a competency, and a `fear` with its `protects`). The twelve sort into **four motivational families**, each answering a fundamental orientation to existence, arranged on two axes (independence↔belonging, stability↔mastery, per Mark & Pearson, corresponding to Jung's Ego/Order/Social/Freedom). The families keep the twelve from being a flat list — they encode *which existential problem* each archetype is a solution to.

**Structure in full.** The four families and their three archetypes each — *Core desire · Strategy · Gift · Basic fear.*

**Family 1 — Independence / "Yearning for paradise" (Freedom; provide fulfillment, seek the true self):**
- **The Innocent** (a.k.a. Child, Utopian, Saint). *Desire:* to be happy, safe, in paradise. *Strategy:* do things right; hold faith and optimism. *Gift:* trust, hope, simplicity, faith. *Fear:* being punished for doing something wrong; corruption. The pure-hearted believer; can be naive, in denial, dependent.
- **The Sage** (Thinker, Philosopher, Scholar, Mentor-as-knower, Oracle). *Desire:* to find the truth; to understand. *Strategy:* seek information and knowledge; think, reflect. *Gift:* wisdom, intelligence, discernment. *Fear:* being deceived, misled, ignorant. The wise counselor; can be dogmatic, over-analytical, ivory-tower.
- **The Explorer** (Seeker, Wanderer, Pilgrim, Adventurer). *Desire:* freedom, to find out who they are through exploring the world. *Strategy:* journey, escape confinement, seek and experience the new. *Gift:* autonomy, ambition, authenticity. *Fear:* entrapment, conformity, inner emptiness. The restless traveler; can be aimless, alienated, unable to commit.

**Family 2 — Mastery / "Leave a mark on the world" (Ego; provide change, exert power):**
- **The Hero** (Warrior, Champion, Rescuer, Soldier, Dragon-slayer). *Desire:* to prove one's worth through courageous, difficult action; to master, triumph. *Strategy:* become as strong and competent as possible; fight for what matters. *Gift:* courage, competence, discipline. *Fear:* weakness, vulnerability, being a coward, "wimping out." The valiant achiever; can be arrogant, always needing another battle, seeing enemies everywhere.
- **The Outlaw** (Rebel, Revolutionary, Wild One, Misfit, Destroyer). *Desire:* revolution, revenge, or radical freedom; to overturn what isn't working. *Strategy:* disrupt, destroy, shock, break the rules. *Gift:* liberation, radical honesty, willingness to burn the corrupt down. *Fear:* being powerless, trivial, ineffectual; being co-opted. The transgressor; can slide into crime, self-destruction, nihilism (the dark version = the criminal, the terrorist).
- **The Magician** (Wizard, Visionary, Alchemist, Shaman, Innovator, Catalyst). *Desire:* to understand the fundamental laws and use them to transform reality; to make dreams real. *Strategy:* develop a vision and *live* it; find win-win transformations. *Gift:* transformative power, healing, catalytic vision. *Fear:* unintended negative consequences; manipulation gone wrong. The transformer; can become the manipulator, the con-artist, the one who plays god.

**Family 3 — Belonging / "Connect to others" (Social; provide connection, seek intimacy):**
- **The Lover** (Partner, Friend, Intimate, Sensualist, Aesthete, Spouse). *Desire:* intimacy, connection, sensual pleasure, to be in a relationship with people/work/surroundings they love. *Strategy:* become ever more attractive, devoted, passionate. *Gift:* passion, commitment, appreciation, gratitude. *Fear:* being alone, unwanted, unloved. The devoted romantic; can lose identity in the beloved, become jealous, please-to-be-loved.
- **The Jester** (Fool, Trickster-as-comedian, Comedian, Joker, Clown). *Desire:* to live in the moment with full enjoyment; to lighten the world. *Strategy:* play, make jokes, be funny, break rules through wit. *Gift:* joy, presence, the ability to speak truth through humor, cutting egos down to size. *Fear:* being bored or boring. The life of the party / the wise fool; can be frivolous, cruel-in-jest, escapist, wasting life on trivia.
- **The Everyman** (Regular Guy/Gal, Orphan, Realist, Citizen, Good Neighbor, the Common Man). *Desire:* to belong, to connect with others as an equal. *Strategy:* develop ordinary solid virtues, blend in, be down-to-earth. *Gift:* realism, empathy, lack of pretense, the common touch. *Fear:* standing out, putting on airs, being rejected or left out. The relatable everyperson (the reader's surrogate); can lose self to fit in, be cynical or a victim (the Orphan face). *(Pearson's original "Orphan" leans harder into the wounded-realist who has lost innocence and learned the world is hard — the survivor's empathy.)*

**Family 4 — Stability / "Provide structure to the world" (Order; provide safety, exert control):**
- **The Caregiver** (Nurturer, Helper, Altruist, Parent, Saint, Supporter). *Desire:* to protect and care for others. *Strategy:* do things for others; give, nurture, sacrifice self. *Gift:* compassion, generosity, self-sacrifice. *Fear:* selfishness, ingratitude; others coming to harm through one's neglect. The selfless nurturer; can become the martyr, the smotherer, the enabler (see `type-typologies.md`'s Enneagram 2).
- **The Ruler** (Leader, Sovereign, King/Queen, Boss, Aristocrat, Judge, Administrator). *Desire:* control; to create a prosperous, ordered community/family/self. *Strategy:* exercise leadership, take responsibility, impose order. *Gift:* responsibility, sovereignty, stewardship, competence-at-command. *Fear:* chaos, being overthrown, losing control. The sovereign; can become the tyrant, the controlling authoritarian, the micromanager.
- **The Creator** (Artist, Inventor, Innovator, Musician, Writer, Dreamer, Builder). *Desire:* to create something of enduring value; to give form to a vision. *Strategy:* develop artistic control and skill; imagine and build. *Gift:* creativity, imagination, craft. *Fear:* mediocrity; a mediocre vision or execution; nothing of value coming to be. The maker; can become the perfectionist who never finishes, the tortured artist, the one lost in the work.

**Vivid exemplars.** Innocent: Forrest Gump, Snow White, Buddy the Elf, Dorothy. Sage: Gandalf/Dumbledore (as knowers), Yoda, Hermione, Marcus Aurelius. Explorer: Indiana Jones, Odysseus, Amelia Earhart, Ishmael, Bilbo. Hero: Wonder Woman, Aragorn, Achilles, Mulan, Beowulf. Outlaw: Robin Hood, Han Solo, V (*V for Vendetta*), Tyler Durden, Katniss-as-symbol. Magician: Merlin, Gandalf (as transformer), Prospero, Morpheus (*The Matrix*), Willy Wonka. Lover: Romeo & Juliet, Jack (*Titanic*), Cleopatra, Cyrano. Jester: the Fool in *Lear*, Falstaff, Deadpool, Puck, Loki-as-comic. Everyman/Orphan: Bilbo/Sam, Frodo (Orphan-wounded), Arthur Dent, Katniss (survivor-realist), Winston Smith. Caregiver: Molly Weasley, Samwise, Mary Poppins, Atticus (as protector). Ruler: Mufasa, Aragorn-crowned, Miranda Priestly (dark), Cersei (tyrant-face), T'Challa. Creator: Gepetto, Dr. Frankenstein (dark Creator), Tony Stark (inventor), any tortured-artist protagonist.

**What it gives you for character depth.** A **one-word summons of a complete motivational person**: pick an archetype and you inherit a core desire (→ a terminal goal), a fear (→ a `fear` with a `protects`), a strategy (→ a `defense`), a gift (→ a competency), and a *shadow/dark version* (the failure mode) — a near-complete character seed. The four families guarantee *value-clash by construction* (a Ruler and an Outlaw want opposite things about order; a Caregiver and a Hero prize opposite things about self). It is the interior-archetype system that translates most cleanly into the repo's **drives and worth** layers, because it is *organized by motivation* rather than by cognition (unlike the Jungian-function typologies). And every archetype's **dark/shadow face** (martyr-Caregiver, tyrant-Ruler, criminal-Outlaw, con-artist-Magician) hands you the villain-version for free.

**Combines-with.** Each archetype's *desire/fear/strategy/gift* is almost literally the **drives schema** (`docs/drives-schema.md`): desire → a terminal `goal`'s `serves`; fear → a `fear` (`protects`, `defense`); strategy → the `defense` pattern; gift → a competency. The **four families map onto Schwartz** (`docs/values-and-stakes.md`): Mastery ≈ achievement/power; Stability ≈ security/conformity/tradition; Belonging ≈ benevolence/affiliation; Independence ≈ self-direction/universalism/stimulation. Onto **Panksepp**: Hero raises SEEKING+RAGE, Caregiver raises CARE, Lover raises LUST+CARE, Explorer/Sage raise SEEKING, Jester raises PLAY, Innocent lowers FEAR. Onto **HEXACO**: Ruler/Hero high-X + drive; Sage/Creator high-O; Caregiver/Everyman high-A; Outlaw low-H-and-A. The **dark faces** map onto `trait-models.md`'s low-H region and `shadow-and-clinical.md`. It sits alongside `type-typologies.md`'s Enneagram (which also types motivation) — many pairs align (Caregiver≈2, Ruler≈8/1, Sage≈5, Everyman≈9, Creator/Individualist≈4, Jester≈7).

**Cautions.** (1) **Branding-cliché saturation.** These twelve are now the lingua franca of marketing decks; used flatly they produce mascot-thin characters ("she's a classic Hero brand"). Use the desire/fear *mechanism*, not the label. (2) **Not empirically validated** as a personality taxonomy — it is a Jung-derived developmental/marketing framework, not a psychometric instrument; its structure has not been factor-confirmed. (3) **Label drift** (Everyman/Orphan/Regular Guy differ in emphasis — the Orphan is *wounded*, the Regular Guy is *content-ordinary*; pick deliberately). (4) **One-archetype-per-character is a trap** — Pearson's own model is *developmental* (we move through many); a rich character carries a dominant plus a shadow plus an emerging one. (5) The **Barnum risk** (every archetype flatters) — the descriptions are aspirational; write the *cost* and the dark face, not just the gift.

**Sources.** Carol S. Pearson, *Awakening the Heroes Within* (1991) and *The Hero Within* (1986); Margaret Mark & Carol S. Pearson, *The Hero and the Outlaw: Building Extraordinary Brands Through the Power of Archetypes* (2001); Pearson & Marr, *PMAI (Pearson-Marr Archetype Indicator) Manual* (2003). For the Jungian grounding, see the Jung sources above.

## Campbell's mythic roles and Vogler's Eight — the hero's-journey functions

**Name and lineage.** **Joseph Campbell**, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949), synthesized world mythology into the **monomyth** — a single deep pattern of the hero's departure, initiation, and return — and named recurring figures the hero meets (the herald of the call, the mentor/supernatural aid, the threshold guardians, the shape-shifting temptress, the shadow/ogre, the trickster, the goddess, the father). **Christopher Vogler**, a Disney/Hollywood story consultant, distilled Campbell into a practical screenwriting manual, first as the legendary in-house "**A Practical Guide to** *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*" memo (1985), then *The Writer's Journey: Mythic Structure for Writers* (1992; 3rd ed. 2007), codifying **twelve stages** and — crucially for this file — **eight character archetypes** understood explicitly as **functions**, not fixed types. Campbell drew on Jung; Vogler drew on Campbell and Jung and made the roles *dramaturgical*. This is the most influential character-role framework in modern screenwriting (Star Wars, The Lion King, The Matrix, and countless others were built on or read through it).

**Core idea (the load-bearing move).** Vogler's decisive reframing: an archetype is **"a function performed temporarily by a character to achieve certain effects in a story"** — *not* a rigid character label. A single character can *wear several archetypal masks* over a story (an ally reveals herself as a shapeshifter, then a shadow), and a single archetypal function can be *shared* across several characters. The archetypes are, Vogler says, best understood as *facets of the hero's own personality* dramatized as separate figures, and as *flexible narrative functions*. This is exactly the repo's insistence that a narrative role is **scene-local and reassignable**, decoupled from the fixed dimensional interior. Ask of each: *what psychological function does this figure serve, and what dramatic function?*

**Structure in full.** Vogler's eight archetypes — *psychological function · dramatic function.*

- **Hero** — *Psych:* the ego seeking identity and wholeness; the part of us that grows, sacrifices, learns. *Dram:* the audience-identification figure who *learns and grows*, who must be willing to *sacrifice* for others, and whose want/need drives the plot. Variants: willing vs. reluctant hero, anti-hero, tragic hero, catalyst hero (who changes others without changing much themselves), group-hero. The Hero's defining act is *sacrifice* — giving up something for a larger good.
- **Mentor** (the Wise Old Man/Woman; Campbell's "supernatural aid") — *Psych:* the Self, the higher/wiser part, the conscience; the gods within. *Dram:* to **teach, train, test, gift, and motivate** the hero across the threshold — provides wisdom, a magical gift, or a needed kick. Often a former hero. Can be *dark* (a false mentor who misleads), *fallen* (a mentor who lost the way), *comic*, or *shamanic* (guiding between worlds). The Mentor typically cannot make the journey *for* the hero and often must be removed (Obi-Wan dies) so the hero stands alone.
- **Threshold Guardian** — *Psych:* our inner demons, neuroses, and the "watchers at the gate" of every new stage; the tests that block growth. *Dram:* to **guard the thresholds** and *test* the hero's resolve at each boundary of the special world — not the main villain but the obstacle at the door (the bouncer, the gatekeeper, the first challenge). Their function is to be *overcome, outwitted, or won over* — and often *absorbed* (a defeated guardian becomes an ally). They signal "you are not ready — prove you are."
- **Herald** — *Psych:* the call to change; the announcement that the winds have shifted. *Dram:* to **issue the call to adventure** and set the story in motion — delivers news, a challenge, a summons that disrupts the ordinary world and makes the adventure unavoidable. Can be a person, an event, a letter, a force. Often appears once, early, then vanishes (the messenger, the wounded man who staggers in with news, the droid with a message).
- **Shapeshifter** — *Psych:* the Anima/Animus, the projected contrasexual other; the unstable, unknowable quality in relationship; our own shifting nature. *Dram:* to **introduce doubt and suspense** — a character whose *loyalty and true nature are uncertain*, who seems to change, whose allegiance the hero (and audience) cannot pin down. The femme fatale, the possible traitor, the ambiguous love interest, the double agent. Their function is *tension and misdirection* — is she friend or foe? The shapeshifter is the archetype most obviously a *mask worn temporarily* (a trusted ally *becomes* a shapeshifter the moment doubt is cast).
- **Shadow** — *Psych:* the repressed, the dark side, the monster within; the disowned self. *Dram:* the **antagonist / villain / enemy** — the force of *opposition* the hero must defeat, embodying the death-wish, the negative, or the road not taken. The most powerful shadows are *humanized* (they have a point; they mirror the hero) and represent *what the hero could become* if they fail. The Shadow can be internal (the hero's own doubt, addiction, dark impulse) as well as external.
- **Ally** — *Psych:* the unexpressed, unused parts of the self; the helpful inner voices personified. *Dram:* the **companion(s)** who serve, aid, challenge, provide comic relief, humanize the hero, and do the work the hero can't do alone — the sidekick, the sworn companion, the team, the loyal servant, the animal helper. Allies *voice the hero's conscience or doubts*, *test ideas in dialogue*, and *share the load*.
- **Trickster** — *Psych:* the mischief-maker and agent of change; the ego-puncturing energy; the desire for transformation through disruption. *Dram:* to **provide comic relief and cut egos down to size** — to *lighten the tone*, expose absurdity, catalyze change through mischief, and keep the story (and the hero) from taking itself too seriously. Often a sidekick or a comic villain; the clown, the wit, the mercurial mischief-maker. (See the cross-cultural trickster section — Vogler's Trickster is the mythic Trickster made a story-function.)

*(Vogler also discusses secondary/derived figures — the Threshold, the Tempter/Temptress, the shadow's henchmen — but the eight above are the canonical set.)*

**Vivid exemplars** (from the mythology every screenwriter shares — *Star Wars* being Vogler's own worked example): **Hero:** Luke Skywalker, Frodo, Katniss, Simba, Neo, Moana. **Mentor:** Obi-Wan/Yoda, Gandalf, Haymitch, Rafiki, Morpheus, Mr. Miyagi, Dumbledore. **Threshold Guardian:** the cantina thugs and the Death Star gate, the Black Rider at the ferry, the Sphinx, Cerberus, the doorman who says "you can't come in." **Herald:** R2-D2 with Leia's message, Gandalf arriving at Bag End, Effie announcing the reaping, the letter from Hogwarts, Hagrid at the door. **Shapeshifter:** Han Solo (initially untrustworthy), Lando Calrissian (betrays then redeems), Gollum, Severus Snape (loyalties uncertain to the end), the classic femme fatale (Brigid in *Maltese Falcon*), Cypher in *The Matrix*. **Shadow:** Darth Vader/the Emperor, Sauron, President Snow, Scar, Agent Smith, Voldemort. **Ally:** Han & Chewie & the droids, Sam & the Fellowship, Ron & Hermione, Trinity, the whole heist crew. **Trickster:** Han Solo (also trickster-coded), the droids' bickering, Loki, the Weasley twins, Jack Sparrow, Bugs Bunny, Puck.

**What it gives you for character depth — and for the *cast*.** This is primarily a **casting and plot-function** toolkit, not an interior one — and that is exactly its gift: it tells you *what jobs your story needs filled* (someone to call the hero out, someone to teach, someone to guard the door, someone to doubt, someone to oppose, someone to accompany, someone to puncture) and lets you *assign and reassign* those jobs across your cast scene by scene. The **shapeshifter-as-temporary-mask** is the sharpest tool: it operationalizes betrayal, ambiguity, and the reveal — the ally who becomes the traitor is a *role change*, not a personality change, and the repo can model exactly that (fixed dimensional interior, shifting relationship-function). The **mentor's necessary removal**, the **threshold guardian's absorption into ally**, and the **humanized shadow** are all reusable structural beats. Because the roles are *facets of the hero*, deploying them also *characterizes the protagonist* (the shadow shows what he could become; the ally voices his conscience; the shapeshifter tests his trust).

**Combines-with.** These are **narrative functions**, so they map onto the **dramatic-structure** skill (the twelve stages, the plot machine) and the **relationships** model (each role is an *edge* to the protagonist: mentor-of, opponent-of, companion-of, whose sign can *flip*), **not** onto HEXACO/Panksepp. The **Shadow** connects to Jung's Shadow (above) and to `trait-models.md`/`shadow-and-clinical.md` when you build the villain's *interior* (the role is "opponent"; the *psychology* is a low-H profile + a wound + a self-justification). The **Shapeshifter** connects to Jung's Anima/Animus and to the repo's `view: self`/others-see split (the character *reads* differently than they *are*). The **Mentor** maps to the Sage/Magician of the Pearson twelve and the Wise Old Man of Jung — *interior* Sage, *functional* Mentor. Vogler's "an archetype is a temporary function" *is* the repo's decoupling of scene-local role from fixed interior — cite it whenever justifying that one character can play many roles.

**Cautions.** (1) **Role ≠ personality — the cardinal rule of this section.** Never store "Mentor" or "Shadow" as a character attribute; store it as a *plot/relationship function the scene assigns*. A character *is* a psychology and *plays* a role; conflating them produces flat "the mentor character" who exists only to mentor. (2) **Monomyth over-application and cultural imperialism** — Campbell's "one story" claim overstates universality and flattens the specificity of non-Western myth (the critique is real and important; see the cross-cultural cautions). Not every story is or should be a hero's journey; forcing the template produces derivative structure. (3) **Formula fatigue** — the Vogler/Campbell beats are so widely used that rote application reads as generic "Campbellian" product; use the *functions* flexibly, subvert the *stages*. (4) **The template is protagonist-centric and often male-coded** — Maureen Murdock's *The Heroine's Journey* and others offer deliberate alternatives; the "temptress/goddess" figures especially are dated. (5) These roles say *nothing about interior* — a story cast entirely by function, with no dimensional depth underneath, is a puppet show; the functions tell you the *jobs*, the repo spine builds the *people* who fill them.

**Sources.** Joseph Campbell, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949; 3rd ed. 2008); Christopher Vogler, *The Writer's Journey: Mythic Structure for Writers* (3rd ed., 2007) and the original "Practical Guide" memo (1985); Campbell & Moyers, *The Power of Myth* (1988). For critique: Maureen Murdock, *The Heroine's Journey* (1990); Robert Segal, *Joseph Campbell: An Introduction* (1990).

## Propp's Seven Dramatis Personae — the folktale role set

**Name and lineage.** **Vladimir Propp**, Soviet formalist, *Morphology of the Folktale* (*Морфология сказки*, 1928; English 1958/1968). Analyzing ~100 Russian fairy tales from Afanasyev's collection, Propp made a structuralist discovery: beneath enormous surface variety, folktales are built from a fixed sequence of **31 narrative functions** (abstract plot-actions — "the villain causes harm," "the hero is tested," "the hero is branded," "the villain is punished") that always occur in the same order (though not all appear in every tale). These functions distribute across **seven "spheres of action"** — the seven **dramatis personae** (character-roles). A founding text of narratology and structuralism (influencing Lévi-Strauss, Barthes, Greimas), and the ancestor of every "character role" system since, including Campbell's and Dramatica's.

**Core idea.** A character is defined *not by traits but by their sphere of action* — the set of functions they perform in the plot. The role is a **bundle of plot-functions**, radically decoupled from personality (Propp cared nothing for interior). One character may occupy several spheres (a single figure acts as both donor and helper); one sphere may be filled by several characters; and a character may *shift* spheres. This is the purest statement of the repo's "narrative role = function, not interior" principle — Propp is functionalism with the personality entirely subtracted.

**Structure in full.** The seven spheres of action (dramatis personae):

- **The Villain** — struggles against the hero; performs the *villainy* (the harm/lack that sets the plot going: kidnapping, theft, a curse, murder) and later the *pursuit* and the *struggle*. The source of the disequilibrium the tale exists to repair.
- **The Donor** (Provider) — prepares the hero and *provides a magical agent* — typically after a *test* (the donor tests the hero, and on passing, gifts the tool/object/knowledge). The old woman by the road, the grateful animal, the mysterious stranger with the sword.
- **The (Magical) Helper** — *aids the hero* in the quest: accompanies, rescues from pursuit, solves difficult tasks, transports, transfigures the hero. Distinct from the donor (who *gives*) — the helper *acts alongside*. The talking horse, the loyal companion, the summoned ally.
- **The Princess** (the sought-for person) *and her Father* — the *reward* and the *goal*; the princess assigns difficult tasks, brands/recognizes the hero, exposes the false hero, and is the prize (marriage). Propp pairs her with the father who assigns tasks and punishes — often functionally fused. (The "princess" is a role, not a gender — the sought-for person/prize/object of the quest.)
- **The Dispatcher** — the figure who *sends the hero off* — reveals the lack, articulates the mission, dispatches. Often appears once, early (the king who commands the quest, the dying father, the parent who sends the child to market). Functionally kin to Vogler's Herald.
- **The Hero** (Seeker-hero or Victim-hero) — *reacts to the donor, marries the princess*; departs on the quest, is tested, struggles with the villain, is branded, returns, is recognized. Propp distinguishes the **seeker-hero** (goes off to fix a *lack* — rescue someone) from the **victim-hero** (is himself the victim cast out, whose tale follows *his* suffering and restoration).
- **The False Hero** — *takes credit for the hero's actions or tries to marry the princess* — presents unfounded claims, is unmasked and exposed (usually at the recognition/task stage). The usurper, the boastful impostor, the wicked stepsisters claiming the slipper.

*(Propp's 31 functions are the deeper structure — abstentation, interdiction, violation, reconnaissance, delivery, trickery, complicity, villainy/lack, mediation, counteraction, departure, the donor's test, the hero's reaction, receipt of the agent, spatial transfer, struggle, branding, victory, liquidation of the lack, return, pursuit, rescue, unrecognized arrival, false claims, difficult task, solution, recognition, exposure, transfiguration, punishment, wedding. The seven roles are *who performs which functions*.)*

**Vivid exemplars.** *Star Wars* mapped to Propp (a classic exercise): Villain = Vader/Empire; Donor = Obi-Wan (gives the lightsaber and the Force-training — and tests); Helper = Han, Chewie, the droids, the Force itself; Princess = Leia (the sought-for person, also dispatcher via the message); Dispatcher = R2/Leia's message ("help me, Obi-Wan"); Hero = Luke (seeker-hero); False Hero — weakly present (arguably Han's initial credit-taking, or absent). Fairy-tale canon: *Cinderella* — Villain = stepmother; Donor = fairy godmother; Helper = mice/birds; Princess-role = the *Prince* (the sought-for prize, gender-flipped); Dispatcher = the royal invitation; Hero = Cinderella (victim-hero); False Heroes = the stepsisters (claim the slipper). *Vasilisa the Beautiful* — Donor = the doll/Baba Yaga; Villain = stepmother; etc.

**What it gives you for character depth (and plot).** Propp is a **plot-role checklist** of surgical clarity: it tells you the *functional slots* a quest-narrative needs filled (someone to cause the lack, someone to gift the tool, someone to aid, someone to send, someone to seek, a prize, an impostor) and — because roles are *pure function* — it makes explicit that **one character can fill several slots and slots can be reassigned**. It is the cleanest possible demonstration of the repo's role/interior decoupling. For generating or diagnosing quest and fairy-tale structure (and much of fantasy, adventure, and heist plotting), it is a fast structural x-ray. The **donor's test → gift** and the **false hero's exposure** are reusable beats. And the **seeker/victim hero split** is a useful early fork (does the protagonist go *toward* a lack, or *recover* from being cast out?).

**Greimas's actantial model — Propp generalized to six actants.** A. J. Greimas (*Sémantique structurale*, 1966) abstracted Propp's seven roles into a more general, genre-independent scheme of **six actants** arranged in three binary axes — *actants* being structural *positions* (fillable by a person, a collective, an abstraction, or an object), not characters: **Subject ↔ Object** (the *desire* axis — the hero and the value/person they pursue: Luke → destroy the Death Star / rescue Leia), **Sender ↔ Receiver** (the *communication/mandate* axis — who dispatches the quest and who benefits: the Rebellion / Leia's plea → the galaxy), and **Helper ↔ Opponent** (the *power* axis — the forces that aid vs. hinder: Han, Obi-Wan, the Force ↔ Vader, the Empire). Because it collapses Propp's Donor/Helper/Dispatcher into cleaner poles and drops the folktale-specificity, it fits *any* goal-driven narrative (a courtroom drama, a romance, a boardroom fight), not just wonder-tales — the reason narratology reached for it after Propp. Same discipline: an actant is a *slot the scene assigns*, one character can occupy several (a Subject who is also their own Opponent — the self-sabotaging protagonist), and the Object can be abstract (justice, love, the truth). Use it as the *most general* structural x-ray in this file when Propp's fairy-tale slots feel too narrow.

**Combines-with.** Pure **narrative function** — maps to the **dramatic-structure** skill (the 31 functions are a plot grammar) and the **relationships** model (each role is a functional edge). Propp's roles align loosely with Vogler's (Dispatcher≈Herald; Donor+Helper≈Mentor+Ally; Villain≈Shadow; False Hero has no clean Vogler equivalent but relates to the Shapeshifter/rival), and with Dramatica's (Villain≈Antagonist; Helper≈Sidekick/Guardian). It says *nothing* about interior — build the *person* who fills each slot from the repo spine (a Villain-role filled by a low-H psychology with a wound; a Donor-role filled by whatever old scholar you like). Greimas's actantial model (above) is Propp abstracted to six actants across three axes — the more general, less genre-bound version, and the bridge from folktale roles to *any* goal-driven plot.

**Cautions.** (1) **Genre-bound.** Propp's morphology was derived from Russian *wonder-tales* specifically; it fits quest/fairy-tale structure well and other genres poorly — do not force a psychological novel or a slice-of-life onto the 31 functions. (2) **Zero interior.** By design it ignores personality entirely — it is a *plot* tool; a character built only from a Propp role is a plot-token, not a person. (3) **The "Princess" and gender** — the labels are archaic and gendered; read them as functional roles (sought-for person, prize, dispatcher) and de-gender freely. (4) **Rigidity of order** — Propp claimed the functions always occur in fixed sequence; later narratology (and any non-folktale) shows this is a property of the *corpus*, not of narrative in general. (5) **Reassignment is the feature, not a bug** — resist the urge to force one-character-one-role; the power is in the many-to-many mapping.

**Sources.** Vladimir Propp, *Morphology of the Folktale* (1928; 2nd Eng. ed., Univ. of Texas Press, 1968); A. J. Greimas, *Structural Semantics* (1966) — the actantial-model generalization (Subject/Object, Sender/Receiver, Helper/Opponent); Alan Dundes' introduction to the Propp translation; Claude Lévi-Strauss's structuralist critique/dialogue.

## Dramatica's Eight Archetypal Characters — the objective-role dyads

**Name and lineage.** **Melanie Anne Phillips & Chris Huntley**, *Dramatica: A New Theory of Story* (1990s; software and book, storymind.com). Dramatica is an ambitious, idiosyncratic, comprehensive story-structure theory that models a complete story as a single **"Story Mind"** — the whole narrative as a model of a single problem-solving mind, with characters representing its **facets**. Within its huge apparatus, the **eight archetypal characters** are its most portable idea: the *objective characters* (the players in the plot's logistics) are built from sixteen **character elements** (Pursuit/Avoid, Logic/Feeling, Control/Uncontrolled, Faith/Disbelief, etc.), and the eight archetypes are the *cleanest bundlings* of those elements into four complementary **dyads**. Dramatica insists the archetypes are a *simplification* — a "starting point" — and that richer characters are built by *redistributing* the elements (a "complex character" who has, say, Pursuit but also Doubt).

**Core idea.** A complete story-argument requires certain **functions** to be dramatized, and they come in **opposed pairs** — the theory's core claim is that drama is the *balanced collision* of these paired motivations. Each archetype is a bundle of four **motivation elements**, and each has a mirror-opposite whose absence would make the story feel one-sided. The eight are best understood as the **objective, plot-level roles** (what each does in the story's logistics), separate from the *subjective* Main Character / Impact Character throughline (who the story is emotionally *about*) — a distinction Dramatica draws sharply and that is genuinely useful: the **Protagonist role ≠ the emotional-POV character**; they *often* coincide but need not.

**Structure in full.** The eight, in their four dyads — *the function · its motivation element(s).*

**Dyad 1 — the drivers of the plot:**
- **Protagonist** — the *prime mover* toward the story's goal; embodies **Pursuit** (and Consideration). The one whose effort *drives the plot forward*, who pursues the goal and represents the effort to *achieve* it. (Note: the Protagonist is a *plot function* — the pursuer — not necessarily the character we identify with. Dramatica separates this from the "Main Character.")
- **Antagonist** — directly *opposed* to the Protagonist and the goal; embodies **Avoid / Prevent** (and Reconsideration). The force working to *prevent* the goal's achievement, to restore the old order, to stop the change. Represents the story's *reluctance to change*.

**Dyad 2 — the reasoning of the story (how decisions get made):**
- **Reason** — decides and acts from **Logic**; calm, rational, controlled; keeps feelings out of choices; the cool head. Represents the *intellect* of the Story Mind.
- **Emotion** — decides and acts from **Feeling**; passionate, reactive, frenzied or warm; responds from the heart, disregarding logic. Represents the *passion* of the Story Mind. (Reason and Emotion are the story's *decision-making poles* — the head/heart split externalized as two characters.)

**Dyad 3 — the confidence of the story (self-assurance vs. doubt):**
- **Sidekick** — **Faithful, supportive, confident**; loyal to (usually) the Protagonist; embodies unshakable belief and support. Represents *self-confidence / faith*. The believer who never wavers.
- **Skeptic** — **Disbelieving, opposing, doubting**; challenges, criticizes, disbelieves; the cynic who questions everything. Represents *self-doubt / disbelief*. The naysayer. (Sidekick and Skeptic are the story's *confidence poles* — faith vs. doubt personified.)

**Dyad 4 — the conscience/temptation of the story (moral pull):**
- **Guardian** — a **teacher/helper/protector** who aids the Protagonist and represents a **moral standard / conscience**; embodies **Conscience** (and Help). The good angel on the shoulder; guides toward the right path. (Distinct from Vogler's Mentor by its explicit *moral* function — the Guardian is the story's *conscience*.)
- **Contagonist** — **hinders and deludes** the Protagonist, *tempting* toward the wrong path or delaying the right one; embodies **Temptation** (and Hinder). *Dramatica's signature contribution* — the Contagonist is **not** the Antagonist (who *opposes the goal*); the Contagonist *tempts and obstructs the hero's approach*, the bad angel on the shoulder, the seducer toward error. (Guardian and Contagonist are the story's *moral poles* — conscience vs. temptation.)

*The sixteen underlying elements* (the finer grain, in eight opposed pairs) — Pursuit/Avoid, Consideration/Reconsideration, Logic/Feeling, Control/Uncontrolled, Faith/Disbelief, Support/Oppose, Conscience/Temptation, Help/Hinder — are *redistributed* to build **complex characters** who break the neat archetypal bundles (a Protagonist who also carries Doubt; an Antagonist with a streak of Conscience). This element-redistribution is Dramatica's mechanism for going *beyond* the eight into individuated characters.

**Vivid exemplars.** *Star Wars* (Dramatica's own worked case): Protagonist = Luke (pursues the goal); Antagonist = the Empire/Vader (prevents it); Reason = Leia (or Obi-Wan — cool, strategic); Emotion = Chewbacca/Han (passionate, reactive); Sidekick = the droids/Chewie (faithful support); Skeptic = Han Solo early ("hokey religions and ancient weapons are no match for a good blaster" — the doubter); Guardian = Obi-Wan (moral teacher, conscience); **Contagonist = Darth Vader as *tempter*** (distinct from the Empire-as-Antagonist — Vader *tempts* Luke to the dark side: "join me"). *The Contagonist is the clearest example* — the figure whose job is *temptation and delay*, not goal-opposition. *Lord of the Rings:* Protagonist = Frodo; Antagonist = Sauron; Guardian = Gandalf; Contagonist = Gollum (tempts, deludes, delays — *and* ultimately serves the goal by accident); Sidekick = Sam (faith incarnate); Skeptic = Boromir (doubts the plan); Emotion/Reason across the Fellowship.

**What it gives you for character depth (and ensemble balance).** Dramatica's gift is the **dyadic completeness check**: it names *eight functions a full dramatic argument wants dramatized*, in *four opposed pairs*, so a cast is "balanced" when both poles of each relevant dyad are present — miss a pole and the story feels one-sided. Two contributions are genuinely distinctive: (1) the **Contagonist** — separating *goal-opposition* (Antagonist) from *temptation/hindrance of the hero's path* (Contagonist) is a real analytic gain that most role systems collapse; the tempter-who-isn't-the-villain (Gollum, the seductive false friend, the addiction personified) is a precise, useful slot. (2) The **Protagonist-role vs. Main-Character split** — the plot's *prime mover* need not be the emotional-POV character (Nick Carraway is the Main Character; Gatsby is arguably the Protagonist-pursuer), which lets you *decouple who drives the plot from whose heart we're in* — directly relevant to POV design (`narrative-craft`). The **element-redistribution** mechanism is the built-in path from archetype to individuated character.

**Combines-with.** **Narrative functions** again — maps to **dramatic-structure** (the plot/decision machine) and **relationships** (each dyad is an opposition-edge). The **Reason/Emotion** dyad connects to the repo's affect/appraisal split and to `emotion-and-decision` (head vs. heart decision-making). The **Guardian/Contagonist** moral dyad connects to `docs/values-and-stakes.md` (conscience = the moral-foundations pull; temptation = the competing self-interest) and to the self-deception engine (`shadow-and-clinical.md`). The **element-redistribution → complex character** move is Dramatica's version of the repo's "translate the archetype into a dimensional profile, then perturb." Cross-walks to the others: Protagonist≈Propp's Hero/Vogler's Hero; Antagonist≈Villain/Shadow; Guardian≈Mentor(moral)/Donor; Contagonist has *no clean equivalent elsewhere* (its unique value); Sidekick≈Ally/Helper; Skeptic≈a doubting Ally.

**Cautions.** (1) **Idiosyncratic and jargon-heavy** — Dramatica's full theory (the "Story Mind," the quad structures, the throughlines) is elaborate, unfalsifiable, and not consensus story-theory; take the *eight archetypes and the Contagonist/Main-Character distinctions* as the portable, useful parts and leave the metaphysics optional. (2) **Role ≠ interior** (as with all narrative systems) — these are *objective plot functions*; don't store them as personality. (3) **The dyadic-balance claim is prescriptive, not descriptive** — plenty of great stories omit poles or blur the pairs; use it as a *checklist to consider*, not a rule to obey. (4) **The eight are explicitly a simplification** — Dramatica itself says archetypes are training wheels; the theory's *point* is the redistributed complex character, so don't stop at the neat eight. (5) The Protagonist/Main-Character split, while powerful, is easy to *over*-apply — in most stories they *do* coincide, and separating them without reason just confuses.

**Sources.** Melanie Anne Phillips & Chris Huntley, *Dramatica: A New Theory of Story* (1993; rev. eds.) and the Dramatica theory book online (storymind.com); Dramatica software documentation (Write Brothers). The eight-archetype and character-element material is in the "Objective Characters" chapters.

## Classic narrative patterns — the stock roles the well keeps recasting

**Name and lineage.** Not one framework but the **shared folk-taxonomy of character roles** that runs through drama from Greek theater to the writers' room — the *stock characters*, *type roles*, and *archetypal figures* that recur so persistently they've been named and re-named in a dozen traditions: the *commedia dell'arte* masks (16th c. — the crafty servant Arlecchino, the boastful Capitano, the miserly Pantalone, the pedant Dottore, the lovers), Northrop Frye's *Anatomy of Criticism* (1957) character-types across the four mythoi, TV Tropes' vast catalog, and the everyday craft vocabulary of "the mentor," "the femme fatale," "the wise fool," "the antihero." These overlap heavily with the systematized sets above (Vogler, Propp, Dramatica) but circulate as *loose, culturally-shared patterns* every reader recognizes. This section gathers the load-bearing ones as a *palette*, with the standing warning that a stock role played straight is a **cliché**, and its life comes from the *specific psychology* the repo builds underneath.

**Core idea.** A stock role is a **reader-recognition shortcut** — a bundle of expectations the audience already carries, which the writer can *satisfy* (fast legibility), *deepen* (the archetype plus a real interior), or *subvert* (the expectation set up and broken). The role is a *surface pattern*; the craft is what you do with it.

**Structure in full — the load-bearing patterns.**

*Hero-family:*
- **The Hero / Champion** — the courageous protagonist who acts for others; sacrifice is the signature (see Pearson's Hero, Vogler's Hero).
- **The Antihero** — a protagonist *lacking* conventional heroic virtue (morally grey, selfish, cynical, criminal, or cowardly) who nonetheless holds the protagonist's role and often our sympathy. The dominant modern hero-mode. *Exemplars:* Walter White, Tony Soprano, Deadpool, Han Solo (early), the Man with No Name, Jaime Lannister, Michael Corleone.
- **The Tragic Hero** — the noble figure undone by a **hamartia** (fatal flaw/error) and often *hubris*; his fall evokes pity and fear (Aristotle's *Poetics*). *Exemplars:* Oedipus, Macbeth, Othello, Anakin, Jay Gatsby, Okonkwo (*Things Fall Apart*).
- **The Byronic Hero** — brooding, charismatic, flawed, rebellious, haunted by a secret past; the sexy melancholic outsider. *Exemplars:* Heathcliff, Mr. Rochester, Severus Snape, the Phantom, many vampire leads.
- **The Everyman** — the ordinary reader-surrogate thrust into extraordinary events (Pearson's Everyman). *Exemplars:* Bilbo, Arthur Dent, Winston Smith, most audience-POV protagonists.
- **The Chosen One** — marked by prophecy/destiny for a unique role (a hero-variant with a fate attached). *Exemplars:* Harry Potter, Neo, Paul Atreides, Buffy, Anakin.

*Villain-family:*
- **The Villain** — the antagonist who *chooses* harm (see Vogler's Shadow, Dramatica's Antagonist). Sub-patterns: the **Dark Lord** (embodied cosmic evil — Sauron, Voldemort), the **Mastermind** (cold planner — Moriarty, Hans Gruber, Littlefinger), the **Tyrant** (the Ruler's dark face — Commodus, Joffrey), the **Corrupter/Tempter** (Dramatica's Contagonist — Mephistopheles, the serpent), the **Anarchist/Agent-of-Chaos** (the Joker, Loki-as-villain), the **Sympathetic/Tragic Villain** (a wound and a point — Killmonger, Magneto, Frankenstein's creature, Thanos), and the **Femme Fatale / Homme Fatal** (seduction as weapon — see Shapeshifter). *The repo builds all of these as interior:* a low-H disposition + a wound + a self-justification (`shadow-and-clinical.md`), with the *pattern above* being only the surface silhouette.
- **The Foil** — not a villain but a character whose *contrast* illuminates the protagonist (opposite traits throw the hero into relief). *Exemplars:* Draco to Harry, Laertes to Hamlet, Watson to Holmes (contrast-foil).

*Guide-family:*
- **The Mentor / Sage / Wise Old Man** — teacher and guide (Vogler's Mentor, Jung's Wise Old Man, Pearson's Sage). Sub-patterns: the **Dark/False Mentor** (Fagin, a corrupting teacher), the **Fallen Mentor** (a broken former hero — Haymitch), the **Reluctant Mentor** (Yoda refusing at first). Signature: often *removed* so the hero stands alone.
- **The Herald** — announces the call (Vogler's Herald, Propp's Dispatcher).

*Companion-family:*
- **The Sidekick / Loyal Companion** — the faithful helper (Vogler's Ally, Dramatica's Sidekick). *Exemplars:* Sam, Watson, Robin, Chewbacca.
- **The Love Interest** — the romantic object/partner (Pearson's Lover as a *relation*); dangerous variants: the **Femme Fatale**, the **Manic Pixie Dream Girl** (a critiqued trope — the quirky girl who exists to enliven the male lead).

*Trickster/Fool-family:*
- **The Trickster** — mischief and change through wit and rule-breaking (see cross-cultural section).
- **The Wise Fool / Jester** — the licensed truth-teller who speaks what others cannot, disguised as folly; the clown with the sharpest insight. *Exemplars:* the Fool in *King Lear*, Falstaff, the court jester, Wamba (*Ivanhoe*), Cervantes' Sancho, Feste (*Twelfth Night*).
- **The Rogue / Lovable Scoundrel** — charming, self-interested, rule-bending, ultimately decent (a trickster-antihero blend). *Exemplars:* Han Solo, Jack Sparrow, Robin Hood, Aladdin, Locke Lamora.

*Other durable patterns:* the **Innocent/Ingénue**, the **Damsel** (and the subverted "damsel who saves herself"), the **Warrior-Woman/Action Girl**, the **Grizzled Veteran**, the **Mad Scientist** (the Creator's dark face), the **Femme/Homme Fatale**, the **Gentle Giant**, the **Mentor's Betrayer**, the **Prodigal**, the **Scapegoat**, the **Martyr**.

*The classical root — Frye's four comic stock-types (from Greek comedy):* beneath the modern catalog lies the oldest systematized role-set in the Western tradition, the four *character* types Aristotle and the Tractatus Coislinianus named and Northrop Frye (*Anatomy of Criticism*, 1957) revived as the machinery of comedy — and they come as **two opposed pairs** that generate, respectively, the comic *action* and the comic *mood*. **The eirōn** — the *self-deprecator*, who understates and feigns ignorance (Socratic irony is named for him); the tricky, modest figure who wins by wit and lets the braggart hang himself (the clever hero, the deadpan underdog, Bilbo, Columbo, Jim Halpert). **The alazṓn** — his opposite, the *impostor/braggart*, self-inflated and unaware of his own inferiority; the boaster, the pompous pretender, the miles gloriosus (Falstaff-as-braggart, Malvolio, Michael Scott, the blowhard about to be deflated). *The contest of eirōn vs. alazṓn is the engine of comic plot* — the self-aware puncturing the self-important. The second pair sets the *tone:* **the bōmolóchos** — the *buffoon*, whose job is festivity, whose only aim is to raise a laugh (the fool, the drunk, the merry clown, the comic-relief sidekick), and **the agroîkos** — the *churl/rustic*, the straight-man killjoy who resists the fun (the miser, the spoilsport, the literal-minded stiff, the "no fun allowed" authority). Buffoon and churl *polarize the comic mood* between festivity and refusal. This quartet is the ancestor of the *commedia* masks, of Vogler's Trickster and Ally, and of half the sitcom cast; naming it exposes the *oppositional* logic modern stock-roles inherit but rarely make explicit.

**Vivid exemplars.** Covered inline above — the point of this section is that these names are *shared currency*: say "Byronic antihero with a fallen-mentor" and any collaborator sees the silhouette instantly.

**What it gives you for character depth.** The **fastest shared-vocabulary casting layer** — these are the patterns readers *already carry*, so naming one gives instant legibility and a set of expectations you can then satisfy, deepen, or subvert. Their real craft value is as **subversion targets**: the damsel who rescues herself, the mentor who's the true villain, the Dark Lord with a sympathetic wound, the comic sidekick who turns out to be the mastermind — each derives its punch from the *stock expectation* it breaks. The **antihero** and the **sympathetic villain** are the two most productive modern patterns (moral greyness is the default register now), and both are *built* by the repo's shadow/trait machinery rather than by the label.

**Combines-with.** These are **surface silhouettes** that the repo *fills with interior*: a Villain-pattern gets a `shadow-and-clinical.md` psychology; an Antihero gets a mid-low-H, high-competence profile with a private code; a Byronic Hero gets high-O + high-Emotionality + a wound + a Persona of cool detachment. They cross-walk directly onto the systematized role-sets (Hero↔Vogler/Propp/Dramatica Protagonist; Villain↔Shadow/Antagonist; Mentor↔Mentor/Guardian/Donor; Trickster↔Trickster). The **Foil** connects to the dramatic-structure principle of contrast and to Dramatica's dyads. The subversion move is the repo's "surprising-yet-inevitable" realism test (`docs/generative-model.md`) applied to *reader expectation* rather than to trait-lean.

**Cautions.** (1) **Played straight, every one is a cliché** — the stock role is a *starting silhouette*, and a character who is *only* the silhouette (the wise mentor who only mentors, the femme fatale who only seduces) is a cardboard cutout. Depth comes from the *specific caused interior* underneath. (2) **Some patterns are actively critiqued/harmful** — the Manic Pixie Dream Girl, the "bury your gays," the Magical Negro, the disposable damsel, the disabled/disfigured villain (`shadow-and-clinical.md`'s ethics section): know which patterns carry baggage and either avoid or deliberately, knowingly subvert them. (3) **Subversion is now itself a convention** — "the twist is the mentor is evil" is expected in some genres; subversion isn't automatically fresh. (4) **Role is not interior** — same rule as every narrative-role section. (5) The **antihero can curdle into edgelord** and the **sympathetic villain into apologia** — hold the moral truth-view visible (`shadow-and-clinical.md`).

**Sources.** Northrop Frye, *Anatomy of Criticism* (1957) — the character-types of the four mythoi and the four comic stock-types (eirōn / alazṓn / bōmolóchos / agroîkos), which Frye draws from Aristotle and the *Tractatus Coislinianus*; Aristotle, *Poetics* (hamartia, the tragic hero); the *commedia dell'arte* mask tradition; Georges Polti, *The Thirty-Six Dramatic Situations* (1895); Christopher Booker, *The Seven Basic Plots* (2004) — archetypal plots and their casts; TV Tropes (as a descriptive folk-catalog, not a scholarly source).

## Cross-cultural archetypes — the recurring figures of world myth

**Name and lineage.** The comparative-mythology tradition — the claim that certain character-figures and motifs recur across unrelated cultures, whether by diffusion, shared psychology (Jung's collective unconscious), or shared narrative logic. Key figures: **Adolf Bastian** ("elementary ideas," 19th c.), **James Frazer** (*The Golden Bough*, 1890 — the dying-and-rising god, the sacred king), **Carl Jung** & **Erich Neumann** (archetypes; the Great Mother), **Joseph Campbell** (the monomythic hero), **Georges Dumézil** (the trifunctional Indo-European hypothesis), **Claude Lévi-Strauss** (mythemes, the structural trickster/mediator), **Paul Radin** (*The Trickster*, 1956), **Lord Raglan** (*The Hero*, 1936 — a 22-point hero-pattern), **Otto Rank** (*The Myth of the Birth of the Hero*, 1909), and **Lewis Hyde** (*Trickster Makes This World*, 1998). The **Aarne–Thompson–Uther (ATU)** tale-type index catalogs recurring *plot*-types across cultures (Cinderella = ATU 510A appears in ~hundreds of cultures). The universality claims are contested (see cautions); the *recurring figures themselves* are undeniable and richly generative.

**Core idea.** Across cultures, storytellers independently arrive at a small set of powerful character-figures because they answer universal human situations — the need for a culture-bringer, a boundary-crosser, a nurturing-and-devouring source of life, a self-sacrificing renewer, a wise elder, a paired opposition. Whether the cause is inherited psyche, cultural diffusion, or convergent narrative pressure, the *figures* are a deep well the writer can draw on with the confidence that readers everywhere half-recognize them. The most cross-culturally robust are below.

**Structure in full — the great recurring figures.**

- **The Trickster** — the *most* cross-culturally universal archetype, and the richest. A boundary-crosser and rule-breaker who is neither good nor evil but *amoral and generative*: he lies, steals, shape-shifts, gluttonizes, lusts, and blunders — yet through his transgressions *brings culture to humanity* (fire, language, art, mortality itself) and *keeps the cosmos from freezing into dead order*. Associated with **thresholds, crossroads, doorways, travel, theft, and speech**. *The world roster:* **Coyote** and **Raven** (Native American — Raven steals the sun/light for humanity; Coyote's appetites remake and misshape the world), **Anansi** the spider (Akan/West African & Caribbean — owner of all stories, wins them from the sky-god by wit), **Eshu/Elegua/Legba** (Yoruba/Fon — the crossroads god, divine messenger and interpreter, who *must* be honored first because he governs all passage and possibility), **Loki** (Norse — the shape-shifting mischief-maker whose tricks both save and doom the gods, father of monsters, agent of Ragnarök), **Hermes** and **Prometheus** (Greek — the thief-messenger of boundaries; the fire-stealing benefactor of humanity), **Sun Wukong** the Monkey King (Chinese — the irrepressible rule-breaking trickster tamed into a pilgrim), **Māui** (Polynesian — fishes up islands, snares the sun, steals fire, dies trying to defeat death), **Reynard the Fox** and **Till Eulenspiegel** (medieval European — the fox who humiliates the powerful; the peasant prankster), **Br'er Rabbit** (African-American — the weak outwitting the strong, a survival-wisdom trickster), **Kokopelli**, **Kitsune**, **Iktomi** (Lakota spider), **Nasreddin Hodja** (the wise-fool of the Islamic/Turkic world). Radin, Jung, and Hyde read the Trickster as the *collective shadow* (the disowned appetites of a whole culture, licensed in story) and as the spirit of *creative disorder* — "the origin, moving spirit, and endpoint of culture" (Hyde). *The one figure that appears in nearly every mythology.*

- **The Great Mother** — the *dual* source of life: **nurturing** (Demeter, Isis, Gaia, Parvati, the Virgin as *Theotokos*, Pachamama) *and* **devouring/terrible** (Kali, Hecate, Baba Yaga, Tiamat, Lilith, the witch of the fairy tale). Neumann's central insight: the maternal archetype is *always both* — the womb and the tomb, the feeder and the swallower — because life-giving and death-dealing are the same power seen from two sides. The nurturing mother, the smothering mother, the wicked stepmother, the earth goddess, the crone.

- **The Wise Old Man / Sage / Mentor-figure** — the elder who dispenses meaning, wisdom, and magical aid (Jung's Senex / mana-personality): Merlin, Tiresias, Odin-as-wanderer, the Taoist immortal, the desert hermit, the shaman, the guru. Cross-cultural because every culture needs to dramatize *transmitted wisdom*. (Its shadow: the *Senex* as rigid, withering authority — see Beebe in `type-typologies.md`.)

- **The Hero / Culture-Hero / Chosen One** — the figure who undertakes the great deed, founds the people, slays the monster, or brings the boon. **Lord Raglan's 22-point pattern** (mysterious/divine birth, attempt on the infant's life, spirited away and raised in secret, return, victory over a king/beast/giant, marriage, reign, eventual fall, mysterious death, holy sepulchre) recurs across Oedipus, Moses, Theseus, Arthur, Jesus, Krishna, and dozens more; **Otto Rank** modeled the birth-of-the-hero pattern (Freudian read); Campbell's monomyth is the departure-initiation-return version. The **culture-hero** (Prometheus, Maui, Quetzalcoatl, Väinämöinen) brings a gift/technology to humanity.

- **The Divine Twins / the Paired Opposites** — the two brothers or companions, often one mortal one divine, one light one dark (the Vedic Ashvins, Castor & Pollux, Romulus & Remus, Jacob & Esau, the Hero Twins of the Popol Vuh, Gilgamesh & Enkidu). Dramatize complementarity, rivalry, and the split self.

- **The Dying-and-Rising God / the Sacrificed Renewer** — the figure whose death and return embody seasonal, agricultural, or spiritual renewal (Osiris, Dionysus, Persephone's descent, Tammuz/Dumuzi, Baldr, Adonis, Quetzalcoatl, and — in the tradition Frazer controversially placed alongside them — Christ). The self-sacrifice that renews the world; the descent to the underworld and return (the *katabasis* — Inanna, Orpheus, Odysseus, Aeneas, Persephone). Underwrites the resurrection/rebirth beat of countless hero-arcs.

- **Others in the well:** the **Scapegoat** (the one who carries and expels the community's sin — Girard's scapegoat mechanism), the **Femme Fatale / Temptress** (Circe, Delilah, the siren, the succubus), the **Wild Man / Shadow-Beast** (Enkidu, the Green Knight, Caliban, the werewolf), the **Fool/Holy Fool** (Nasreddin, the yurodivy, Parzival-as-innocent), the **World-Renouncer/Ascetic**, the **Sacred King** (whose vitality *is* the land's — the Fisher King).

**Dumézil's trifunctional hypothesis (a structural cross-cultural pattern).** Georges Dumézil argued Indo-European myth and society organize around **three functions**, often personified as three figures/gods/classes: (1) **Sovereignty/sacred** — the priest-king, law and magic (Odin/Varuna/Jupiter; the Brahmin); (2) **Force/martial** — the warrior (Thor/Indra/Mars; the Kshatriya); (3) **Fertility/production** — the farmer/nurturer, wealth and health (Freyr/the Ashvins/Quirinus; the Vaishya). A ready-made *three-figure cast* (ruler, warrior, provider) that recurs across Indo-European story and maps cleanly onto ensemble design. *(See `worldbuilding-frameworks` for the trifunctional hypothesis in society-building.)*

**Vivid exemplars.** Covered inline (the roster *is* the exemplar-well). For modern recastings: **Trickster** — Loki (Marvel), Q (*Star Trek*), the Genie (*Aladdin*), Tyrion Lannister (wit-as-survival), Bugs Bunny, the Cheshire Cat. **Great Mother** — Galadriel (nurturing) vs. the devouring mother in horror; Mother Gothel (smothering); the AI "Mother" tropes. **Divine Twins** — Luke & Leia, the Weasley twins, Loki & Thor, Cain-and-Abel recastings. **Dying-and-rising** — Gandalf (falls, returns as the White), Aslan, Neo, Superman (*Death and Return*), Jon Snow. **Culture-hero** — Prometheus recast as every "brings forbidden knowledge/fire" figure (Oppenheimer-as-myth, the AI-creator).

**What it gives you for character depth.** The **deepest, widest well of pre-charged figures** — reach in for a Trickster, a devouring Mother, a dying-and-rising renewer, and you inherit a figure readers *across cultures* half-recognize, carrying millennia of resonance you can evoke, deepen, or subvert. The **Trickster** especially is a gift for the project: an amoral boundary-crosser who generates change and speaks forbidden truth is a superb *catalyst* character (and a `PLAY`+`SEEKING`-heavy, low-conscientiousness, high-Openness dimensional profile). The **dual Great Mother** and the **katabasis/descent** are structural depth-charges for arc and backstory. And **Dumézil's three functions** hand you an instant, culturally-grounded *ensemble* (sovereign + warrior + provider). Cross-cultural sourcing also *de-parochializes* your palette — the well is far larger than the Western hero.

**Combines-with.** These feed *both* repo channels. As **interior**, they translate to dimensional profiles (Trickster = high-O/PLAY/SEEKING, low-conscientiousness, low-conformity; Great Mother = high-CARE with a devouring shadow; Sage = high-O/knowledge-SEEKING). As **function**, they map to the narrative-role systems (Trickster↔Vogler's Trickster; culture-hero/Chosen One↔Hero; Great Mother/Sage↔Mentor/Guardian). They ground the **worldbuilding-frameworks** skill's mythology/religion layer (pantheon design, the trifunctional society) and the **dramatic-structure** skill's rebirth/descent beats. The Trickster-as-collective-shadow connects to Jung's Shadow and `shadow-and-clinical.md`. Dumézil's three functions cross-link to Schwartz value clusters (sovereignty≈power/tradition; force≈achievement/power; fertility≈security/benevolence).

**Cautions.** (1) **The universality claim is genuinely contested and can do harm.** Campbell's monomyth and Jung's archetypes *overstate* cross-cultural sameness and *flatten* the specificity of individual traditions; scholars (Lévi-Strauss on Campbell, folklorists generally) warn that "it's all one story" erases what makes each myth *its own*. Use the figures as a *generative palette*, not as a claim that all cultures secretly tell the same tale. (2) **Diffusion vs. archetype vs. convergence is unresolved** — recurrence may be inheritance, borrowing, *or* independent invention; don't assert a metaphysical cause. (3) **Cultural appropriation and flattening** — lifting a specific sacred figure (Eshu, Coyote, Kali) out of its living tradition and into generic "trickster/goddess" content can be reductive or offensive; handle living religious figures with specificity and respect, and prefer *understanding the particular* over homogenizing it. (4) **Frazer's dying-and-rising-god category is disputed** — many scholars argue it over-generalizes (some "dying gods" don't cleanly rise; the pattern was partly retrofitted); cite it as an evocative motif, not settled comparative fact. (5) **The Western hero-pattern is not the human default** — Raglan/Campbell are *Indo-European-and-Mediterranean-weighted*; many traditions center different figures (the trickster, the ancestor, the community) — draw widely, not just from the hero-monomyth.

**Sources.** Paul Radin, *The Trickster: A Study in American Indian Mythology* (1956, with Jung & Kerényi commentaries); Lewis Hyde, *Trickster Makes This World* (1998); Erich Neumann, *The Great Mother* (1955); James Frazer, *The Golden Bough* (1890); Georges Dumézil, *Mitra-Varuna* (1948) and the trifunctional writings; Lord Raglan, *The Hero* (1936); Otto Rank, *The Myth of the Birth of the Hero* (1909); Joseph Campbell, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949); the Aarne–Thompson–Uther tale-type index. For critique: Robert Ellwood, *The Politics of Myth* (1999); Alan Dundes on comparative method.

## How to reach into this file

- **First, decide which kind of archetype you need — interior or function — because they translate differently.** If you want to *conjure a whole person fast* (their motive, gift, fear, shadow), reach for an **interior** system: **Jung's four** (for the *architecture of one self* — mask, shadow, inner-other, wholeness), the **Pearson/Mark twelve** (for a *motivated character-seed* that drops straight into the drives schema), or a **cross-cultural figure** (for a pre-charged, resonant type). Then **dissolve the label into the dimensional store** — HEXACO leanings, Schwartz/MFT weights, Panksepp baselines, drives — because that is what the decision engine resolves (`docs/generative-model.md`). If you want to *cast a story* (who does what to the plot), reach for a **functional** system: **Vogler's eight** (mythic roles), **Propp's seven** (folktale/quest slots), or **Dramatica's eight** (dyadic completeness + the Contagonist). Store these as **relationship/plot functions the scene assigns**, never as personality.

- **Never conflate role with interior — the load-bearing discipline.** A character *is* a psychology (the fixed dimensional store) and *plays* a role (scene-local, reassignable). The ally who becomes the traitor is a **shapeshifter role-change**, not a personality change — same HEXACO, same drives, new function. Model betrayal, ambiguity, and the reveal as *role reassignment over a stable interior*. Vogler's "an archetype is a temporary function" is the warrant; the repo's role/interior decoupling is the implementation.

- **Match the system to the job.** Interior architecture and self-conflict → **Jung** (Persona/Shadow/Anima/Self; individuation as the arc). A motivated character in one word → **Pearson twelve** (desire/fear/strategy/gift → drives). A resonant, culturally-deep figure → **cross-cultural** (Trickster, Great Mother, dying-and-rising, Dumézil's three-function ensemble). Casting a hero-journey → **Vogler**. X-raying a quest/fairy-tale plot → **Propp** (or, for *any* goal-driven plot regardless of genre, **Greimas's six actants**). Checking a cast for dyadic balance, or naming the tempter-who-isn't-the-villain → **Dramatica** (the Contagonist). A fast shared silhouette to satisfy or subvert → **classic patterns** (antihero, Byronic hero, sympathetic villain, wise fool).

- **Mine the dynamics, not just the boxes.** The load-bearing gifts are the *motions*: Jungian **individuation** (integrate the Shadow/Anima toward the Self) as the master arc; the Pearson archetypes' **dark/shadow faces** as free villain-versions; the **shapeshifter's mask-change** and **mentor's removal** as plot beats; Dramatica's **Contagonist** (temptation vs. opposition) as a precise slot; the Trickster's **generative disorder** as a catalyst. Feed these to `docs/arc-engine.md` (individuation, the dark-face turn) and `docs/relationships.md` (role-edges that flip).

- **Stay honest about the science and the universality.** The depth-psychological archetypes (Jung, Pearson) are *unfalsifiable and unvalidated* as psychology — generative and communicative, not evidentiary. The narrative roles (Vogler, Propp, Dramatica) are *craft frameworks*, not empirical claims, and can *over-apply* (not every story is a monomyth). The cross-cultural universality claims are *contested and can flatten* real traditions — use the figures as a palette, never as "all cultures tell one story," and handle living sacred figures with specificity and respect. When a character or narrator *believes* in an archetype, that belief is characterization; the repo does not endorse the metaphysics. For measurement, the repo defaults (HEXACO et al.) are the backbone; the archetypes are the well laid over it — conjure and cast from the well, then build the caused, particular person underneath.
