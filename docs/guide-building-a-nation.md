# Building a nation — the sheet and the gap list

**Status: PROCEDURE.** Written 2026-10-01. `guide-building-a-world.md` walks a blank page to a world, and
`guide-building-a-cast.md` builds the people in it. Neither says what a built *people* holds, or how to see what a
people you have already written is missing. This does, for a nation: a kingdom, a tribe, a city-state, a league of
towns, a people with no state at all. Anyone with a name for themselves counts. `guide-building-a-settlement.md` does
the same for one place.

It is the method, not a nation: examples are invented, so this file stays upstreamable to the template. A book's own
filled sheets belong with the book.

---

## The one thing to know before you start

A nation is not its part in the story. A people drawn only through the pressure the story puts on it (its war, its
curse, its tyrant) is a stage set: strike every line about that pressure and nothing is left. A recognizable nation
has an ordinary day, an ordinary complaint and an ordinary pleasure that would still be there if the story never
happened. The test at the end checks exactly that.

## Two ways to use it

- **To build.** Fill it in order, part by part.
- **To check.** For a people you have already written, go through the sheet and point each line at the note that
  answers it. A line with nothing to point at is a gap. What you lack, you add.

## What a built nation is: fourteen parts, a list of names, and the test

Each part is answered in short rows, one fact a row. The number in brackets is the layer that fills it, in the order
a society is built (below), so a session that builds one layer fills one part.

0. **At a glance, and the count.** Its name, and what outsiders call it; its people; where it lies; what it is known
   for; one sentence a local would agree with. The count: how many people; households; the share by broad age; the
   share by trade; town against country; how many under arms. Filled first, and checked again after every layer.
1. **The ground** [1]. Land, climate, water; what is grown, dug, caught; the animals kept, what pulls the loads, what
   people fear in the wild; whatever special force the world has (magic, a technology, a curse) and what it makes easy
   or hard here; what the land was like two generations ago and what is running out.
2. **Food and work** [2]. A normal day's food and a bad year's; who grows, catches and carries it; on what terms people
   work (for wages, bound to the land, owned, drafted, in a guild); the trades and their tools; what nobody here can
   make and must buy; the pay, from the lowest to the highest; everyday prices; two household budgets that add up.
3. **Money, markets, roads and places** [3]. The coin and what backs it; weights, measures and distances; markets and
   trade inside the nation; roads and rivers, days from the capital to the border, what stops in winter; who sends
   what to whom; the gazetteer, one line per place (`guide-building-a-settlement.md`), marking which places get a full
   sheet.
4. **Household, body and property** [4]. Who lives under one roof; the house itself (built of what, heated and lit
   how, water from where); marriage, children, old age; what is men's work and women's, and who may own, inherit,
   fight, rule or speak in court; inheritance; who owns land, tools and houses; widows and orphans; births, what people
   die of and how young; who heals, and what it costs.
5. **Power, law and force** [5]. Who rules, how they are chosen, why people obey; how an order reaches a village;
   taxes; courts, punishments, who enforces; where the law does not reach (crime, the black market, what it costs to
   look away); what ordinary people can do about any of it. *Force:* who bears arms, how they are raised, paid and
   fed, who commands them, what that costs the fields, how it stands to whoever rules. "Nobody rules" is an answer: say
   then what holds people together. End the part with the named offices and institutions.
6. **Belief and ritual** [6]. Gods, or their absence and what stands in for them; birth, marriage and death; holy
   days; what is sacred and what is forbidden, each with what breaking it costs; who keeps the rites and what they live
   on.
7. **Neighbours** [7]. With each neighbour: what crosses the border both ways, war, tribute, marriage, migration,
   spying; the border itself, who guards it and who slips across; what each says of the other. Written in pairs (see
   the sessions, below).
8. **Memory, records and time** [8]. Who can read; schooling; who writes what down and where it is kept; the script;
   the calendar (how years are counted, the seasons, the hours of the day); the history it tells itself; what it has
   forgotten or hides.
9. **Customs and pleasures** [9]. Greetings, manners, taboos, each with its cost; eating and drinking; dress; sayings;
   games, songs, stories, sport, what children play; what the rich do for pleasure that the poor cannot.
10. **Who "we" are** [10]. What makes someone one of them; the tongue they speak and who else understands it; markers
    of class and origin (how names work, speech, dress, food); outsiders who live among them and how they are treated;
    who "they" are.
11. **Shocks** [11]. The last great disaster and what it left; the next one building; what would break the nation and
    who would suffer first.
12. **History in eras** [after the ladder]. Each era told as growth, strain, crisis, turning point, fallout and memory:
    what really happened, and what each side remembers. Hero and traitor are names memory gives people afterwards.
13. **The lines of strife** [last]. Collected, not written fresh: every part's winners and losers, and the live
    tensions history leaves, gathered into the factions, what each wants, the reason each gives, and who wins and
    loses from the way things are now.

**Appendix: the names it uses.** Its coins, units, offices, titles, institutions, festivals, foods and tools, each in
its own tongue, with the part that coined it. Every layer adds to it. It is the seed of the book's vocabulary and the
names-already-used list the cast guide asks you to carry.

## The rules every sheet keeps

- **One row, one fact,** with an id and its tag first: `N5.3 [open: the tax scene] who collects the salt tax`.
- **Tag every row with where it stands:**

  | tag | means |
  |---|---|
  | `[C note "a few words copied from it"]` | canon: the note, and words copied from it, so a script can check the words are there |
  | `[D]` | derived: it follows from canon by a step the row states |
  | `[P session]` | proposed by a session and not yet adopted. It stays in your session records, out of the book, until you adopt it; then it becomes `[C]` |
  | `[open: what needs it]` | nobody has said yet; name the scene, person or part that will lean on it. The open rows are the to-do list |
  | `[≠ C a / C b]` | two canon notes disagree. Settle it yourself; a session cannot |

- **Plain words,** the way someone who lives there would say it.
- **Every custom names what breaking it costs,** and every hard rule carries the reason the people who keep it would
  give. No villains for evil's sake.
- **Every arrangement names its winners and its losers.**
- **Numbers sit in sums that close:** the count's shares add up, the budgets balance, the gazetteer sums to the count.
- **One real-world analog per part,** named from `.claude/skills/worldbuilding-frameworks/`, not one per row.
- **Every row earns its place:** a draft could contradict it, or a character could do something with it. Lists of
  sayings and superstitions with no cost and no user are scenery; keep them short.
- **Cite, never copy.** Facts between two nations (populations side by side, rates of exchange, trade, wars, borders)
  live once, in one note about the world's contacts, and both sheets point at it. A people's own facts live in its
  notes, and the sheet points at them.

## The order, and how deep to go

The parts run in the order a society is built, each needing the ones before it: markets need a surplus, a surplus
needs food, laws need someone with the power to make them. Part 0 first; then 1 to 11; then 12, once the present
stands; then 13, last. Pay belongs to part 2 and the coin to part 3: price part 2 in the coin the canon already names,
or in days of the staple food, and restate it once part 3 fixes the coin.

**Depth follows the story.** Fill the full sheet for the people the story lives among. For the others, the short form
(parts 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 7 and 10, with the gazetteer) is enough until a scene or a character leans on more. "Open" is
the honest state of most rows for a background people; each open row says what would need it, and that is the order
to fill them in.

**What reaches the engine.** Most of a sheet is reference, read by you and by the sessions. What a scene must use goes
into the world note's live fields, by the table in `guide-building-a-world.md` ("it becomes"). A filled sheet is a
note of `type: concept`, never untyped; write what only the author may know (part 8's forgotten truth, part 12's real
history) between a `%% truth %%` line and a `%% /truth %%` line.

## How to run the sessions

- **One part per session.** A session that fills three parts fills three thin ones.
- **Give the session the sheet so far,** every filled row with its tag, the open rows of the part it is filling, and
  the world's fixed facts.
- **Write neighbours in pairs.** One session writes what crosses between two peoples, both ways, once; the two sheets'
  part 7 rows agree on every fact and differ only in what each says of the other.
- **Write each session up the way the cast guide says:** the writer invents nothing; anything that breaks the canon is
  left out and flagged; unsettled rows stay open with the alternatives named.
- **After each session,** update part 0's count, add the new names to the appendix, and recount the open rows.

## The test

1. **The stage set.** Strike every row that mentions the story's central pressure. From what is left, set three
   scenes on an ordinary day, in a kitchen, a market and a court, with named food, a coin, a custom and a complaint.
   If the sheet goes blank, it was a set.
2. **Three sums.** *Fed:* the land and its yield feed the people, with a bad year's margin named. *Paid:* the lowest
   wage covers a household's food and rent, or the sheet says who goes short. *Armed:* the people under arms are a
   share the fields can spare, and the tax that pays them shows in someone's budget.
3. **Two strangers.** Two writers each describe a farmer's, a soldier's and a widow's day from the sheet alone. Where
   they differ on a fact, that row is open, whatever its tag says.
4. **Neighbours agree.** What crosses each border balances both ways.
5. **The floor.** Every canon row's words are in its note; every custom has a cost; every open row says what needs
   it; every number sits in a sum that closes.

## What usually goes wrong

- **The stage set.** Every custom, trade and grievance turns out to be the premise in another hat. The first test
  catches it.
- **Lists without use.** Twenty sayings, ten superstitions, no cost and nobody who acts on them.
- **Money with no scale.** Each session invents its own wages and prices, and they do not agree. Settle one scale per
  people in part 2, and the rates between peoples in the contacts note, before anyone compares.
- **Everyone lives in a city.** A gazetteer of big towns that sums to the whole population leaves nobody on the land
  that feeds them. Part 0's town-against-country share catches it.
- **Copies drift.** The same fact written into two sheets will soon say two things. Cite.
